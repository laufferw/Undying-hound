# **Chapter 3: Go to Ground**

Ling Mei read the bamboo slip three times, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder. Then she set it face down on the floor, which was as close to defiance as a slip of bamboo could get.

"Hour of the snake tomorrow," she said. "That's a day and a half."

*A day and a morning,* Romeo said from under the sleeping platform. *Do not round up. Rounding up is how cultivators die.*

"What happens at an assessment?"

*That depends on the elder. In my experience, a beast is set on a measuring stone, and the stone tells the elder whether the beast has qi, how much, and of what kind. An ordinary dog makes the stone sulk. An awakened beast makes it light up like a lantern festival.*

"And you'd light up."

*I would flicker,* Romeo said, with some bitterness. *At first level, I am a very small lantern. But a flicker is enough to get a dog put in a cage.*

Outside, the morning bell rang over the training grounds, three slow strokes. Ling Mei flinched at it, and he smelled why: four years of being late meant four years of punishments, and her body remembered every one.

*Go,* he said. *Chant your Azure Wolf Breathing with the others. Move your lips, but do not do the breathing. Your channels have turned one percent toward the sea, and if you force them back to the pond in front of a hundred disciples, you will be sick on somebody's shoes.*

"Whose shoes?"

*Choose wisely. And on your way back, steal me some bitterroot.*

She paused at the door. "Steal?"

*Borrow without asking from an herb garden that will not notice. The green kind, with the purple stalk. I will explain later.*

She went. Romeo listened to her footsteps squelch up the ditch until they were gone, then turned to the floorboards, and every one of his ancestors sat up inside him and said: *Finally.*

---

The boards were old pine, laid loose over packed earth, and the third one from the wall had warped in the damp until there was a gap beneath its end the width of two of his toes. He worked his nose into the gap and pushed. The board lifted a finger's breadth and dropped back with a clack.

He tried again with his shoulder, then with his whole long body, getting his head and both front legs under the board and heaving upward like a caterpillar trying to lift a house. On the fourth try the board slid sideways and fell flat, and Romeo stood in a narrow trench of darkness, breathing the smell of cold dirt, and was happier than he had been since the lightning.

He dug.

There is a way that dachshunds dig which other dogs, given a thousand years, could not learn. The front paws are broad and turned slightly out, and they go like a pair of shovels driven by a mill wheel, left-right-left-right, while the deep chest wedges into the hole and the back legs kick the spoil out behind in a steady spray. The ears flop forward. The tail stands straight up, a little white-tipped flag that says to the world: *I am busy. I have found the badger. Leave me alone.*

There was no badger. There were roots, and a broken pot that someone had buried a century ago, and a quantity of small stones that he spat out one by one with grave dignity. There were three worms, whom he let go. By the time the light through the torn window had turned from grey to yellow, he had a hole as deep as he was long, angled under the platform where nobody standing in the room could see into it.

By noon he had turned it into a proper den: a short entrance shaft, a bend, and a round chamber at the bottom just large enough for a small dog to curl in, nose to tail, with the floor above him. By early afternoon he had begun the second tunnel, the back door, angling it toward the ditch behind the hut. Every den needed two ways out. A badger had taught him that, once, by coming in the second one.

It was in the round chamber, resting with his muzzle on his paws and his coat full of dirt, that he noticed the smell.

It was very faint. Wet stone. Iron. And under them a slow, heavy sweetness, like honey left in a cold room, that he had not smelled since he was very young and had never once forgotten.

Earth qi.

Not much. A thread, a hair, a trickle so thin that no sect surveyor with spiritual sense would ever have bothered to mark it. It ran beneath the hut from somewhere up the hill, following the same seam in the stone that carried the rainwater down into the ditch. That was why the floor was always damp. That was why, he suspected, the roof leaked in three places and the hut had been given to the girl nobody wanted: it was a bad, wet, cold spot, sitting on top of the only good thing in the outer quarter.

Romeo put his nose to the bottom of the chamber and breathed in.

The Nine Scents Sutra was not a method for gathering qi from the air. That was only its first layer, the layer a beginner could use. Its true principle was simpler: *whatever has a scent can be followed, and whatever can be followed can be drawn in.* Air had a scent. So did water. So did stone, and the slow breath of the earth moving through it.

He let it come to his nose.

The trickle of earth qi rose to him like a cat that had decided, after some thought, to be friendly. It was cold and dense and it went into his small empty sea like a stone dropped in a well, and then another, and another, and he lay perfectly still in the dark under the floor with his eyes closed and his tail very slowly sweeping the dirt, and did not count the time.

---

Ling Mei came back in the middle of the afternoon, grey in the face, holding a bundle of bitterroot in a fold of her sleeve. She stopped in the doorway and stared at the hole in her floor, and the long fan of earth sprayed across her boards, and the small dirty dog sitting proudly beside it.

"You've dug up my house."

*I have improved your house. It now has a cellar.*

"It now has a *pit*."

*A pit with two exits. Also, I am second level. You may congratulate me.*

She sat down heavily on the edge of the platform instead, and the smell of her reached him properly: sour stomach, aching wrists, a hot, crowded pressure along the channels of her arms like a river blocked at a bridge. She had not done the sect breathing. He could tell. But she had stood in the cold for two hours among a hundred people doing it, and her body, after four years, had tried to follow them on its own.

"It hurt," she said. "Not much. Like my hands were full and couldn't let go. I was sick behind the bell tower."

*Not on anyone's shoes?*

"Not on anyone's shoes." A faint smile. "I thought about Zhao Peng's."

*He was not there?*

"Confined to quarters for the pill. His friends were, though. They kept barking at me." She rubbed her wrists. "Is it going to be like this every day?"

*Worse, for a while,* Romeo said, because she was a person who would rather be told the truth. *Then better, all at once. The tide is turning. Turning tides are rough water.*

She nodded slowly. Then she held out the bitterroot. "So what's this for?"

Romeo took a stalk in his teeth and chewed. It was exactly as foul as he remembered, a green, numbing bitterness that crawled across his tongue and down his throat and into his channels, where the qi he had gathered all day stopped moving and lay still, sluggish, like fish under ice.

*My first master used this for toothache,* he said, his mind-voice slightly thick. *He would chew it and spit it on a cloth and hold it to his jaw. When I was very young, just awakened, I ate some off his cloth because I eat everything. For half a day afterward I could not feel my own qi at all. Neither could anything else.*

Ling Mei's eyes widened. "So tomorrow, you chew this, and the stone sees an ordinary dog."

*So tomorrow, I chew this, and the stone sees an ordinary dog,* Romeo agreed, *who is also very slightly drunk. It will have to do. But first I want to see the stone.*

---

He went at dusk, by the back door.

The new tunnel came out among the reeds on the ditch bank, and from there the ditch ran uphill, a narrow channel of brown water between stone walls, past the outer disciples' quarter and the kitchens and the training grounds, all the way up to the Beast Hall on its terrace near the top of the slope. Every bit of rain that fell on the hill came down that ditch. Every bit of wolf smell came with it.

Romeo did not walk up the path. On the path there were disciples, and disciples had eyes. He went up the ditch itself, belly low, his short legs paddling in the deep places and his long body sliding through the reeds like an otter's. Twice he heard voices passing above him. Neither time did anyone look down. People never look down. It was the single greatest advantage of being small, and the main reason he had survived his first two hundred years.

The ditch ended at a stone drain under the Beast Hall's lower kennels, barred with an iron grate. The bars were spaced to stop a wolf. They were not spaced to stop a dachshund who was prepared to breathe out all the way, flatten his ears, and pour himself through sideways one rib at a time.

He came up through a gutter channel into the kennels, dripping, and the smell of wolf hit him like a wall.

There were twenty of them, perhaps more, in pens along both sides of a long stone hall. Big grey and blue-grey spirit wolves, each one as large as a pony, each one wearing a thin collar of black iron etched with contract runes. Most were asleep. The ones that were awake did not get up. They lay with their heads on their paws and watched him with yellow eyes, and none of them growled, and that, more than anything, made the fur along his spine stand up.

A bound beast did not growl unless it was told to.

*You smell like a hole in the ground,* said a voice in his head.

It came from the last pen on the left. The wolf in it was older than the others, grey gone almost white at the muzzle, one ear torn. His collar had worn a bald ring in the fur of his neck.

*I live in one,* Romeo said. *It is very comfortable. You speak.*

*All of us can, a little. They prefer we don't.* The old wolf's eyes moved over him, the small body, the short legs, the dirt. *You're the girl's dog. The one that stole the pill.*

*I returned the pill to its rightful owner,* said Romeo. *In a manner of speaking. What is your name?*

A long pause. *The kennel-book says Grey Nine. Before that I had another, but it was a long time ago.*

*Then I will call you Grey for now, and you may tell me the other when you like.* Romeo looked down the hall to where it opened into a larger chamber lit by a single lamp. *I came to see the stone.*

Grey was silent for a moment. Then: *Don't touch it.*

*I have no intention of touching it.*

*It's not one of ours,* the old wolf said. *The sect's old stone was cracked. That one came a month ago. The Elder brought it back from the third tier, wrapped in cloth, and he doesn't let the stewards handle it. Two weeks ago a fox was brought in, a wild one, found in the bamboo. It had awakened on its own. No master.* The yellow eyes held his. *It touched the stone. The stone said its name out loud. Its true name, the one it gave itself. And the Elder wrote the name down on a slip, and sealed the slip, and sent it up the mountain with a runner. The next morning the fox was gone and its pen smelled of nothing at all. Not even fox.*

Romeo did not answer. He walked, very softly, down the length of the hall, keeping to the shadow under the pens, and into the lamplit chamber.

The stone sat on a low wooden altar in the centre of the room. It was smooth and grey and about the size of a melon, and around its middle ran a single thin band of bright metal.

He did not need to go closer. He could smell it from the doorway. Under the lamp oil and the wolf musk, under the dust and the rain and his own wet coat, the band gave off a cold, clean, patient scent. That morning he had caught the faintest ghost of it on a bamboo slip. He had smelled it full strength exactly once before, in the last moment of his old life, as it looped around his neck as gently as a collar.

Silver.

Romeo stood very still in the doorway, and every hair along his long red back rose, and for the first time since he had woken in the mud, the oldest puppy in the world was afraid.

Behind him, in the dark of the kennels, the old wolf said quietly: *The Elder comes down to check on it every night, little hole-dog. You should go.*

Above them, on the stairs from the upper hall, a door opened.

# **Chapter 25: Borrowed Feet**

Romeo did not breathe for the length of the second knock, and then he did, because a dog who does not breathe cannot smell, and smelling was the only thing he had that was worth anything at this hour.

He breathed in slowly, through the nose only, the way Shen had taught him to breathe in a field where a viper might be sleeping.

Earth. Charcoal. The old physician's bitter herbs, already fading. Stoat, which would take a week to fade. Ling Mei asleep above him: warm wool, rice-water, the tired salt of a girl who had walked over a mountain twice in two days. And coming down the open shaft on the cold draught that slid under the hut door and across the floorboards, the smell of the night outside: snow, pine, frozen ditch-water, a far-off whiff of the kennels.

That was all.

That was what frightened him. Someone was standing on the other side of that door. Someone had a hand, or something like a hand, and had used it five times on the wood. The draught came straight from the place where they stood. It should have carried their skin and their breath and the leather of their boots and whatever they had eaten for supper. It should have carried their fear or their patience or their spite.

It carried a hole.

Romeo knew the shape of it now. He had followed it through the dry passage. He had lain in the ice of the lake slot while it went past above him. It was the place in the world where a smell ought to be and was not, and his nose, which had learned in the last days to lean toward missing things, leaned toward it now the way a compass needle leans north.

⟨Ling Mei,⟩ he said, very softly, into the place behind her eyes. ⟨Wake up. Don't move.⟩

He heard her breath catch. The charcoal bowl gave a small tick as it cooled. The cord around her wrist went taut and then, carefully, slack again, which told him she had understood both halves of the instruction.

"Someone knocked," she whispered. She could not speak to him the way he spoke to her, but she had learned to whisper down the shaft as if he were a man standing at the bottom of a well.

⟨Yes.⟩

"Is it..." She did not finish. He could smell what she was not saying. *Is it Bao. Is it someone safe.*

⟨No. Whatever is out there has no smell.⟩

She went so still that he could hear her heart through the floorboards.

Three knocks. A pause. Two.

Then nothing. No voice. No scrape of a boot. Only the draught, and the hole in it, patient as a held breath.

⟨Don't answer,⟩ said Romeo. ⟨Whatever it says. If it says your name, don't answer. If it says mine, don't answer. If it uses the voice of someone you love, especially don't answer.⟩

"It hasn't said anything," Ling Mei breathed.

⟨Then it is waiting to see what we do.⟩

---

There were two ways out of the den. One was up the shaft and through the hut and through the door, which was the way any reasonable creature would go to see who was knocking, and which was therefore out of the question. The other was the back-door tunnel.

⟨Untie the cord,⟩ he told her. ⟨Keep the end. Leave the harness on me.⟩

She did not argue, which was how he knew she was truly afraid. He heard her fingers work at the knot, felt the small tug and release at his ribs, and the long hemp cord came slithering up the shaft and was gone.

"Where are you going?" she whispered.

⟨Under,⟩ said Romeo. ⟨Where it doesn't expect a dog to be.⟩

He got out of the trough the way old men get out of baths, front end first and the rest following under protest. His back did not like it. His back made a long, cold, private remark about it that started between his shoulders and ended somewhere near his tail. He waited for the remark to finish. Then he put his nose into the mouth of the back-door tunnel and went in.

And everything was suddenly, wonderfully easier.

The tunnel was a dachshund's height and a dachshund's width and not a hair more, because a dachshund had dug it. It went level through the dark for forty paces with a little rise at the end, and it did not ask him to climb anything, jump onto anything, or be taller than he was. His short legs, which were so ridiculous on stairs, were exactly the right length here. His long back, which complained at every step and shelf in the world, lay straight and level and held itself up on its own. The earth pressed close on every side and smelled of him.

Three thousand years, and some things never changed. Every badger-digger in his blood knew this: up there, on top of the world, you were a small, strange, low dog that everything could step over. Down here you were the thing in the dark that the badger was afraid of.

He went fast. Even stiff, even with his shoulder aching where he'd bruised it on the mountain, he went fast.

---

He came out among the ditch reeds with snow on his whiskers and lay flat for twenty breaths, reading the night.

The sky had cleared. A waning moon, thin as a fingernail paring, hung low in the east above the black pines, giving almost no light at all. Five nights after this one there would be none. The outer yard was a field of grey snow and darker shapes: the huts in their crooked rows, the long storehouses, the steward's well with its rope frozen stiff. In the centre of the yard, far off, a single lamp burned beside the assessment stone, and two masked shapes stood beside it, very still.

Romeo did not go toward the door of the hut. He went along the ditch on his belly, ploughing a low trench through the crusted snow with his chest, a small red plough at work in the dark, until he was level with the side of Ling Mei's hut. Then he came up out of the ditch and crept along the foot of the wall, where the eaves had kept the snow shallow, with his ears dragging and his belly an inch off the ground.

People looking for a dog look at the height of a dog. Things looking for a dog look at the height of a dog. Nobody looks at the bottom of a wall.

He reached the corner. He put one eye around it.

The doorstep was empty.

He had known it would be. He had felt the hole in the draught begin to drift away while he was still in the tunnel. But he waited anyway, counting, while his heart thumped against the snow, and when nothing moved he crept to the doorstep and put his nose down to it.

Snow. Wood. The iron of the latch. Ling Mei's own smell from a hundred comings and goings. Nothing else at all.

But there were footprints.

---

They came across the yard from the direction of nowhere in particular, ten or twelve of them, crisp in the new snow, and stopped at the step, and turned, and went away again around the side of the hut, the side he had not come by.

They were the prints of straw sandals.

Romeo stood over the nearest one for a long moment without understanding why the hair along his spine had risen all the way to his tail.

Then he understood.

The prints were small, the size of a short man's foot. The toes turned out. The outer edge of each sandal had pressed deeper than the inner, because the man who wore them had walked on the outsides of his feet all his life, the way men do who spend their days on hillsides. And every right print had a short smear behind the heel, where the foot dragged a little before it lifted, because the man had fallen down a gully gathering cloud-ear fungus one wet spring and his right knee had never been quite right again.

Romeo had walked behind those footprints for years. He had walked behind them through mud and dust and frost and spring grass, up a thousand hillsides, at the end of a length of rope and then at the end of nothing at all. He had put his small feet in them when the snow was deep, because it was easier, and Shen had laughed at him for it. He knew them better than he knew his own face. He had not seen them for three thousand years.

He bent and smelled the print, because he could not help it, because some part of him that was still a young dog by a river believed that if he smelled it he would find onions and pine sap and the old cotton of Shen's jacket and the good sour smell of a man who had worked all day.

It smelled of snow.

Only snow. A print with no foot in it. A walk with no walker.

*You read the river,* Romeo thought, and it was not quite mind-speech and not quite a growl. *You read the river, and you found the hat, and you found the man who walked into the water after it, and you took his feet.*

He understood now what Ling Mei had meant. A memory worn like a coat. It had put on Shen's walk the way a man shrugs on a borrowed jacket, and come to knock on the door of the one creature alive who would know it.

And if he had gone up the shaft and through the hut and opened that door, he would have found nobody, and these prints, and he would have followed them. He knew himself. He would have followed them anywhere.

There was one more thing near the doorstep, and he almost missed it, because it was not a smell of the thing that had knocked.

It was across the lane, at the corner of the next hut, where a woodpile leaned against the wall. A man had stood there. Not briefly: long enough for the warmth of his boots to melt two dark ovals into the snow. Romeo crossed to it low and fast and put his nose into the ovals, and the smell came up at him all at once. Lacquer. Leather. Horse. Silver. A soldier's old sweat. And fear, a great deal of fresh fear, the sour kind that comes from watching something you cannot do anything about.

The still escort.

He had been standing here in the dark, watching Ling Mei's door, when the thing with no smell came across the yard on Shen's feet and knocked. He had stood here through all five knocks and the long silence after. And then he had gone, quickly, back toward the guest hall, and his trail was full of the jerk and stumble of a man who keeps looking over his shoulder.

Had he come to warn them? To keep watch for his masters? Romeo stood with his nose in the man's footprints and could not tell. Fear smells the same whichever side you're on.

He turned back to the straw-sandal prints. They went around the far side of the hut, so he followed them, keeping wide of them as if they might bite, his belly brushing the snow.

They went along the wall. They went around the back. They went, without hurrying, along the back wall of the hut until they reached the place, as near as Romeo could judge, directly above the round chamber of his den.

And there they stopped.

Not turned. Not stepped away. Stopped, the left foot and then the right with its little dragging smear, side by side at the foot of the wall, as if the man who wore them had stood there a moment and then simply stepped down into the ground.

Romeo did not think. He lay flat in the snow beside the last print and pressed his chest and the side of his face to the frozen earth, the way he had pressed them to the ice of the lake, and listened through the bones of the mountain.

Ling Mei's heartbeat, above and to his left, quick and light.

The creak of a floorboard as she shifted.

And below her, below the hut, below the den, far down the seam tunnel that ran uphill through the dark to the wet slot and the spring and the pin, something tapped on stone.

Tap.

A pause.

Tap.

It was coming down.

And Romeo, lying in the snow with every hair on end, realised two things at once. The first was that the knock had never been an invitation to open the door. It had been a way to get one small dog out of his hole.

The second was that he had heard that rhythm before. Not only in the dry passage. Long before that. Tap, and a pause, and tap: an old man's digging stick on a hill path, the right knee slow to follow. He had heard it every morning of his first life.

⟨Ling Mei,⟩ he said, and his inner voice did not sound old at all. ⟨Put the board back. Sit on it. Now.⟩

Above him he heard the third floorboard scrape and drop into place.

Below him, the tapping stopped, exactly underneath her.

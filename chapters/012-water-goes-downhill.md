# **Chapter 12: Water Goes Downhill**

Before Bao told him the way down, Romeo asked the other question. He had been carrying it since the bitterroot nearly burned, and he was not going to climb into a river with it still in his mouth.

*A collector is coming,* he said. *The day after the naming. Bitterroot fooled a Nascent Soul twice, and the second time it nearly didn't. I need something better. You've hidden from everything that ever looked for you. How do I hide from them?*

The old turtle took his time, the way he took his time over everything, and the cold took its time over Romeo. His teeth had stopped chattering, which he knew was worse than chattering.

*You're asking the wrong question,* Bao said at last. *A collector doesn't look for dogs. The world's full of dogs. It looks for what's missing.*

*Missing?*

*Every bound beast smells of silver, Romeo. Faint. Under the fur, under the qi. The wolves at your sect will reek of it. You don't. You smell of dirt and stubbornness and that girl, and nothing else. To something that hunts the unbound, you stand out like a lamp in a cellar.*

Romeo thought of the assessment stone with its silver band. Of the thread above his head, humming. Of a scent his nose had filed away without his asking: *Silver (bound). Source: unknown.*

*So I need to smell bound,* he said slowly.

*You need to wear a little of what you hate.* The stone around him shifted, the way an old man's shoulders shift. *I didn't say you'd like that either. I said it was the answer. Now hold your breath. I'm only going to do this once, and I can only feel one toe.*

Somewhere beneath Romeo's paws, something ground open. The air pocket tilted. The water around his hind legs, which had been as still as a bowl, began very gently to pull.

*Where does it go?*

*Down,* said Bao. *Where else does water go? You followed it up, didn't you? Follow it back.*

Romeo understood then, a heartbeat before the pull became a drag and the drag became a hand closing around his whole body. The seam. The thread of earth qi he had followed up the mountain from the trough under Ling Mei's hut, spring by spring, the black pines, the stain on the rock face, all of it fed from here. The lake did not sit on top of the mountain. It drained down through it, along the same crack in the world his nose had walked.

He had time to take one breath, to close his jaws on the knot of the lunch bundle so that it would not strangle him, and to think, with great clarity, *I am a dog, and this is a terrible idea.*

Then the lake swallowed him.

---

At first light, the beast-physician came up the outer-disciple path with a lantern she did not need and a stoat around her neck like a scarf.

Ling Mei had been awake all night. She had used it.

She had rubbed the last stalks of bitterroot along the inside of the shaft until her fingers burned. She had laid her own oldest under-robe at the bottom of it, folded the way a dog folds a thing to sleep on. She had swept the hut, and then unswept it, because a hut with a sick dog in it does not look swept. She had practised the face of a girl who was worried about her dog, and found she did not need to practise it.

The beast-physician was small and very old and had a squint that might have been a squint or might have been her looking at two things at once. She did not give her name. She ducked into the hut, sniffed once, and said, "Hm."

"He's under the floor," Ling Mei said. "He's had the runs since two days ago. He won't come out. He hates people looking at him when he's ill."

"All dogs hate that," said the beast-physician. "So do all elders." She crouched by the third floorboard with a crack of knees and lifted it before Ling Mei could. The smell of sick dog came up, and under it the bitterroot, sharp and green. The old woman's nostrils widened.

"Bitterroot."

"For worms," said Ling Mei. "My grandmother used it."

"Did she." The physician peered into the shaft. It was the width of one small dog and black as a well. "Pup? Pup-pup. Here, now." She clicked her tongue. Nothing answered. She held her hand over the hole, palm down, and Ling Mei felt the faint pressure of qi moving, a beast-physician's touch going down into the dark. It came back, and the squint deepened.

"Smoke," said the physician. "Go and say hello."

The stoat poured off her shoulders like water off a roof and was down the shaft before Ling Mei could breathe. She heard it scrabble at the bend, then nothing.

Ling Mei put her right hand under her thigh, because it had started again, and waited, and did not pray, because she did not know who she would pray to.

It was a long time. Then a sneeze came up the shaft, and another, and Smoke came out of the hole backwards, very fast, sneezing so hard his whole body folded in half. He climbed his mistress's arm, wrapped himself around her neck, and put his face into her collar, and from there made a small, offended chattering.

The beast-physician listened to it with her head on one side. Bound beasts and their masters did not need words; Ling Mei had seen it in the kennels, the way Elder Fang would glance at Brow and know.

"He says," the old woman said, "that there's a bed down there, and a dog smell, and enough bitterroot to kill a horse. And a back door." She turned her squint on Ling Mei. "Into the reeds."

"He goes out to do his business," said Ling Mei. "He's very private."

"And where is he now?"

Ling Mei looked her in the eye. "Doing his business."

For a long moment the only sound in the hut was Smoke muttering into a collar. Then the corner of the beast-physician's mouth moved, very slightly, in a direction that was not quite a smile.

"I'll tell the Elder what I smelled," she said, lowering the floorboard back into place. "Sick dog. Bitterroot. Runs." She stood with another crack of knees and looked down at the hand Ling Mei was sitting on. "I won't tell him what I didn't smell. Not today."

At the door she paused and set a little paper packet on the shelf.

"Warming powder," she said. "In warm water, for a dog that's been somewhere cold. Or for whoever." She did not look at Ling Mei's hand again. "Have him at the naming, girl. Elder Fang can count past one."

Then she was gone down the path with her lantern and her scarf, and Ling Mei sat down very suddenly on the floor, and found that she was laughing, and that her eyes were wet, and that the tide in her was still leaning, ever so slightly, uphill.

---

There was no up. There was no down. There was black water, and stone, and speed.

The outflow beneath Bao was a crack in the bedrock no wider than Romeo was long, and the lake went through it like grain through a funnel and took him with it. He struck a wall with his shoulder. He struck another with his hip. He tumbled, and could not tell he was tumbling except that the stone came from a new side each time. His legs, which had never been long enough for anything, were now entirely useless; there was nothing to stand on and nothing to swim towards. His lungs began to burn.

He did the only thing a dog can do in the dark. He used his nose.

He could not breathe in, so he did not breathe. He let it come to him, the way Shen had taught him on a riverbank three thousand years ago, smacking him on the rump with a bundle of herbs: *Stop chasing it. Stop trying to be tall. Let it come to your nose.* And it came. Even through water, even shut, his nose found the seam. It was the same thread of earth qi he had followed uphill, the clean mineral cold of it, and it ran through this black stone like a vein through a wrist. The water followed it because water followed cracks, and the crack followed the seam, and the seam went home.

He stopped fighting. He stretched himself out, long and low, ears flat, legs tucked, tail straight behind him, and let the current take him lengthwise instead of sideways. His body, which had been the joke of every room he had ever entered, was the right shape for a pipe. He went down the mountain like a sausage down a drain.

His head broke into air.

A gap. A pocket where the channel widened and the roof lifted a hand's breadth above the water. He gulped air that tasted of iron and stone and hung there, paddling his tremendous, useless paddle, and a blue panel unfolded in the dark in front of his streaming eyes.

**\[WARNING\]**  
**\[Condition: Hypothermia (moderate). VIT and AGI reduced while exposed.\]**  
**\[Recommendation: leave the water.\]**  
**\[Time remaining: 1 day, 3 hours.\]**

*Thank you,* Romeo thought at it. *Very helpful. I'll think about it.*

The panel lingered a moment longer than it needed to, as it had at the lake shore, like an old servant who disapproves of where you are going but will hold the door. Then it folded.

Beneath it, Romeo felt something else settle into him: not a level, nothing so grand, but a kind of quiet click deep in his channels, as if a door he had been leaning on had finally admitted it was a door.

**\[Nine Scents Heavenly Tracking Sutra: insight gained.\]**  
**\[A scent can be followed through water.\]**

He would have been pleased, if he had been warm enough to feel his own tail.

The current took him again. Down, and down, and down. Twice more the roof opened and gave him a breath, and both times he took it without thanking anyone. He passed through a place where the water roared and the stone shook with it, and he guessed it was under the long snow shoulder he had tunnelled on the way up. He passed through a place where the water went suddenly warm, and his nose told him *black pines*, and he nearly wept. The spring. The black-pine spring, high on the mountainside. He was a third of the way home in the time it would have taken him to cross the saddle.

Then the channel narrowed, and slowed, and turned, and the water pressed him against the outside of the turn the way a hand presses a coin into a table, and something in the wall there was cold.

Not stone-cold. Lake-cold was one thing and stone-cold was another, and this was a third. Smooth. Too smooth to have been made by water. It pressed against his ribs through his wet fur, and it was humming, very faintly, a single long note he felt in his teeth.

Romeo's nose told him what it was before his mind would let it.

Silk. Lamp smoke.

Silver.

He was pinned against a pin. A shaft of bright metal driven into the living seam of the mountain, as thick as his foreleg, and from its head, running away up the dark channel toward the lake and Bao, a thread so fine he could not see it, only smell it, singing.

Every hair on his back stood up. Everything in him that remembered a chain coming down out of the clouds told him to flinch, to twist, to get his neck away from it. The current would not let him. It held him there, chest to the silver, like a hand on the back of a disobedient dog.

And very close, in the black water, so close that the sound arrived with no echo at all, something tapped on the pin from the other side.

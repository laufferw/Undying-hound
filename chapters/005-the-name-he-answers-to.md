# **Chapter 5: The Name He Answers To**

The Beast Hall by daylight was a different animal from the Beast Hall by night. The upper floor was all polished cedar and tall paper windows, with a long counter where stewards weighed feed and a wall of pigeonholes stuffed with kennel-books. It smelled of ink and lamp oil and, faintly, under everything, of wolf.

Ling Mei walked through it with her chin up and her arms crossed over the front of her robe, and Romeo, riding against her ribs, could feel her heart going like a rabbit's.

*Slower,* he told her. *Breathe out first. You are a disciple bringing her dog to be registered. You are bored. You would rather be eating.*

*I would rather be eating,* she thought back, very clearly, and her heart slowed by a beat or two.

The bitterroot sat on his tongue like a wet leaf on a cold step. His qi lay under it, dull and still, and his mind-voice came out a little thick, as though he were speaking through a mouthful of rice. A dog who could not feel his own qi could not show it to anyone else.

But a name was not qi.

The steward with the ink-stained fingers led them past the counter, down a short passage and through a door, and the smell of wolf came up to meet them at last. They were in the lamplit chamber. Romeo knew it from the other side: the low wooden altar, the single lamp, the stairs he had heard the Elder's footsteps on. By day a high window laid a bar of grey autumn light across the floor and over the stone.

Elder Fang Wuji was sitting behind a small writing table beside it, brushing characters into a ledger. He did not look up.

Brow lay across the doorway to the kennels, filling it. His eyes were open.

"Outer disciple Ling Mei," the steward said, and bowed, and backed out, and closed the door with the speed of a man who had just set down something hot.

---

Fang Wuji finished the character he was writing. He rinsed his brush. He set it on its rest with a small click, and only then did he lift his eyes, and they went past Ling Mei's face entirely and settled on the two floppy red ears hanging over her sash.

"So this is the dog," he said.

"Yes, Elder."

"Put him down, please."

She set Romeo on the floor. He stood on the cold boards on his four short legs, looked up at the Elder with his head tilted and his eyes wide and his whole face arranged into the expression that had extracted dinners from emperors, and wagged his tail.

It was a very good wag. Low, loose, a little foolish. The wag of a dog who did not know what a sect was.

Fang Wuji looked at him for a long moment. Romeo smelled the man properly now, in daylight, without three feet of kennel water in the way. Ink. Clean silk. Pine soap. Patience, which has a smell, a dry one, like paper left in the sun. And under the patience, small and sharp and carefully kept, something Romeo had smelled on a great many cultivators over three thousand years, usually just before they did something unforgivable.

Appetite.

"Zhao Peng tells me," said the Elder pleasantly, "that he is a thief."

"He tells everyone that about everything, Elder."

The faintest movement at the corner of the beard. It might have been a smile. "Discipline Hall agrees with you. Discipline Hall also found a stolen pill of Elder Qiu's at the Zhao boy's feet, which was very convenient for you." He folded his tidy hands. "Where did you get him?"

"He found me, Elder. I gave him a bun."

"A bun." Fang Wuji seemed to taste the word. "Three days ago."

"Yes, Elder."

"Then let us see what three days and a bun have bought you." He nodded at the altar. "Lift him onto the stone. Paws on the stone, if you please. Not your hands. I want to hear the dog."

---

Romeo had spent the night turning the problem over like a bone, and he had come out the far side of it with exactly one idea. It was not a good idea. It was the kind of idea you had at the bottom of a hole, when the badger was coming and the only way out was through.

A name was not a word. A name was the thing you turned toward when it was called.

The name he had given himself, the morning of the peach, was the first thing he had ever owned. He had turned toward it for a long time. But it had been said aloud, in the whole of his life, by exactly one voice: his own.

The other one had been said ten thousand times. At dawn, crossly, when he would not come out from under the blanket. At noon, on the mountain paths, when he had run too far ahead after a hare. At dusk, over a pot of thin millet, with a crust held out. In the last winter, very softly, from a straw bed, over and over, as though the old man were checking he was still there.

*Ling Mei,* he said. *When I am on the stone, call me. Use the name I told you. Do not stop until I tell you.*

She did not ask why. She bent and lifted him, one hand under his chest and one under his long back where it was weakest, the way he had shown her, and set him on top of the stone.

It was cold. It was smooth. His four paws slid on it, and he had to splay them and dig in his claws, and his belly nearly touched the silver band. The cold, clean, patient scent came up into his nose and went straight down his spine, and every hair on his back rose, and his body remembered the chain.

The stone began to hum.

"Romeo," said Ling Mei.

She said it the way you say a dog's name: warm, a little coaxing, rising at the end.

And Romeo, on top of the thing that had come down out of the sky for him, did the hardest piece of cultivation he had done in three thousand years. He did nothing.

He did not reach for his qi. He did not guard his sea. He did not hold onto the old name or push it away, because a thing you push against is a thing you are touching. He only let the girl's voice come to his nose, the way Shen had taught him to let the world come, and under her voice, he let the other voice come too. The old man on the hill path. The crust held out. *Romeo. Romeo. Where've you got to, you long fool.*

His tail began to wag. He did not tell it to. It had never once in three thousand years waited for permission when that name was called.

"Romeo," Ling Mei said again, softer.

The hum rose. The silver band warmed under his belly. Something cool and thin, like the Elder's spiritual sense but older, and much, much more careful, slid in through the pads of his paws and up through his legs and began to feel around inside him, the way a hand feels for a key in the dark.

It found the name it was looking for. It found the one that turned toward it.

*Romeo,* said the stone, in its voice like wind over a jar.

Ling Mei's breath caught. The Elder's eyes did not move.

The cool, careful thing did not leave. It went on feeling. It went deeper, past the tail, past the old man on the hill, down toward the quiet place underneath where a very young dog had once sat under a peach tree and said a word to himself for the first time. Romeo felt it go. He felt it brush the edge of that quiet place, and the stone drew a breath it did not need, and the first sound of a second word began to form in the jar—

And the bitterroot, which he had been holding in his cheek all morning like a very small, very foul secret, went up his nose.

It was not entirely an accident. It was not entirely on purpose either. A dachshund's sneeze is not a small event. It starts in the back of the throat and travels the full length of a long body and comes out the front all at once, and Romeo had been building toward this one for an hour. His head snapped down. His ears flew forward. A great wet spray of green, chewed, bitter pulp went across the top of the stone and over the silver band and halfway up the Elder's left sleeve.

His back paws slipped. He went off the stone sideways, scrabbling, legs everywhere, and Ling Mei caught him against her chest before his long back hit the floor.

The hum stopped. Mid-breath. The stone sat on its altar, dripping.

**\[Name returned: Romeo.\]**  
**\[Note: A name given in love is answered first. This is not a rule. It is merely true.\]**

Romeo, held upside down against Ling Mei's ribs with his legs in the air, read the last line twice. The System did not usually editorialize. He decided to think about that later, too. Later was getting very crowded.

---

Fang Wuji looked at his sleeve.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he took a square of clean cloth from the writing table, wiped the green from the silk, folded the cloth, and wiped the stone with the other side of it, slowly, all the way around the silver band.

"Again," he said.

*Again,* Romeo told Ling Mei, before she could look at him. *Same as before. Call me.*

She set him back on the stone. Her hands were shaking now, and not from fear. He smelled the tide in her arms, the hot, crowded pressure of a river pressing on its old banks, harder in the cold room. It did not show in her voice.

"Romeo."

This time he did not have the bitterroot. This time he had only the one idea. He let it come. Ling Mei's voice. The old man's. The crust. The straw bed in the last winter, and a thin hand on his head, and the word said over and over as though checking he was still there. He filled himself with that name until there was no room left in him for anything else, until he was nothing at all but a small red dog who was being called and was coming.

The stone hummed. The cool, careful thing slid in through his paws and felt around and found him turning toward it, and went no further.

*Romeo,* said the stone. And was silent.

Fang Wuji waited. The bar of grey light moved a finger's width across the floor. The stone said nothing more.

"Romeo," the Elder repeated at last, as if trying it in his mouth. "A strange name. Old-fashioned. Not a name from these mountains." He looked at Ling Mei. "Did you name him?"

"No, Elder. It's what he answers to."

It was perfectly true, and Romeo was proud of her.

"Hm." Fang Wuji laid two fingers on the stone, beside Romeo's front paws, and closed his eyes. The Elder's spiritual sense swept over him once more, thin and cool, and found what the bitterroot had left behind: a dog with less qi in him than a well-fed chicken. The fingers lifted. "No cultivation worth the name. No awakening. A dog who answers to his name." The Elder opened his eyes. "Most dogs do not answer to their names that well after three days, disciple."

"He's very clever, Elder."

"Evidently." He picked up his brush, wet it, and wrote in the ledger: a few neat characters, quickly. Romeo could not read them upside down, and the ink smelled of nothing but ink, which was somehow worse.

"Registered," said Fang Wuji. "One dog, unawakened, to outer disciple Ling Mei. Keep him out of the kennels. My wolves are particular." He looked once more at Romeo, held against her chest, and his voice did not change at all. "And keep him well, disciple. It would be a pity if anything happened to a dog that clever in the next few days."

---

They went out the way they had come, through the cedar room, past the counter and the pigeonholes, out into the cold bright noon and the long, loud line that smelled of chickens. Ling Mei did not run. She walked down the terrace steps at the pace of a bored disciple who would rather be eating, and only when they were around the corner of the training ground wall did she lean against it and let her breath out in one long shudder.

"Was that good?" she whispered. "Tell me that was good."

*It was not bad,* said Romeo. *He has a name for his ledger and no proof for his suspicions. He will come again, but he will have to come with something better than a rock.*

"You sneezed on an Elder."

*I am a dog,* said Romeo, with dignity. *Dogs sneeze.*

She laughed, a cracked, frightened, helpless laugh, and pressed her face into the top of his head, and he let her. Her wrists ached against him. He could smell it. Tonight would be rough water again.

It was then, over her shoulder, that he saw Brow.

The great blue-white wolf had come out onto the terrace. He stood at the top of the steps above the registration table with his pale, iron-striped head turned toward the corner of the wall, and the noon light lay on his bronze collar, and his nose was moving, moving, all the time.

A voice came into Romeo's head. It was deep and slow and very quiet, the voice of something that had not been permitted to use it in years, and it sounded rusty, as a gate sounds rusty.

*Small dog,* said Brow. *The stone began to say a second name. I heard the start of it.*

Romeo went very still against Ling Mei's chest.

*He did not hear it,* said Brow. *His ears are only a man's.* The pale eyes held his across the yard. *But I am bound, and if he thinks to ask me, I will have to tell him.*

A pause, long as a breath held under water.

*He has not asked me yet.*

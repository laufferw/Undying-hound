# **Chapter 37: Kid Leather**

Nobody answered the door. That was the physician's doing; he stood with one arm in his coat and the other out of it and his one good eye on the latch, and did not move, and so nobody else did either.

The knocker waited. He was good at it. He waited the way snow waits on a branch, without any sign of effort, and after a while the latch lifted by itself, because the physician had never in his life locked a door against a sick animal and was not going to begin now.

"Thank you," said the collector, and came in.

He had to bend his head under the lintel. The tack room was not built for a man in a grey riding coat, or for a pale lacquer mask with no mouth in it, and with him inside there was no room left for anything but the brazier, the bench, Romeo on his horse blanket, and three people trying not to breathe too loudly. Smoke had vanished entirely inside the physician's collar. Romeo could feel the stoat's heart going against the old man's throat from across the room.

The collector's left hand was bare.

He held it a little away from his body, palm down, the way a man holds a hand he has just washed and has nowhere to dry. The glove hung from the fingers of his right. It was a long, clean hand, very white, with neat nails and no calluses anywhere, and round the base of the fourth finger ran a thin bright line that Romeo took at first for a ring.

It was not a ring. It was under the skin.

*Silver,* thought Romeo. *Of course it is.*

Then he smelled the rest of it, and his tail, which had been lying quietly along the blanket, went very still.

The collector smelled of what he always smelled of: clean skin, faint silk, lamp smoke, a great deal of silver. His coat smelled of horse and frost. His right hand, inside its glove, smelled of kid leather and the warm salt of a living palm. But from the left wrist down, there was nothing. Not leather, not skin, not the cold of the yard. The heat of the brazier moved toward that hand and bent away from it. The steam off the salt bag bent away from it. It was a place where the smells went *round*.

*I see it,* thought Romeo. *I do not understand it. Those are different things.*

The stranger was on the third floorboard. He had smelled it there not an hour ago, in her voice, with its knees drawn up. It could not be here as well. And this was not the stranger; the stranger was a hole the height of a tall man, and this was a hole the size of a hand, with a man very definitely attached to the rest of it.

"Forgive the intrusion," said the collector. He was looking at Romeo. "Elder Fang mentioned the dog was poorly. I have a weakness for small animals."

"His back," said the physician. His voice was rustier than usual. "Strained in the snow. The Elder wants him kept quiet tonight. I'm to bring him up."

"So I heard." The mask turned toward Ling Mei, and stayed there. "And you are the young lady who has been resting at home all day."

Romeo felt her draw breath. He felt, quite clearly, that she was about to tell the truth, because she always did, and the truth was *I was on a mountain*.

"The steward," said Ling Mei, "says I was."

There was a small pause.

"Yes," said the collector, and it was impossible to tell from his light, pleasant voice whether he was amused. "He does."

He stepped closer to the bench. One step was all there was room for. Romeo lay on his side with the salt bag on his back and looked up at the mask with his head tilted on the blanket, as a dog does when a stranger leans over it: ears soft, eyes wide, a small sausage-shaped animal with no thoughts in it at all. He had looked exactly like this at the feet of emperors.

And under the look, he was counting.

Six levels of qi sat in his channels, dense and quiet from the turning-over in Ling Mei's tide. A week ago it would have been nothing; he would have been as empty as a dog ought to be. Now it was a lamp in a dark room. The collector had not felt it across the yard, or through the door. He would feel it through a hand.

⟨Ling Mei,⟩ said Romeo. ⟨I am going to put it in you again.⟩

He felt her go cold. Last time it had felt to her like swallowing soup the wrong way, and Fang had seen her tide move.

⟨I am asking,⟩ he said. ⟨You can say no.⟩

Ling Mei put her good hand on his scruff, the way she did, the way you hold a dog in a crowd. It looked like nothing; a girl steadying her ill animal on a bench. It was a yes.

He sent it down the bond.

Not pushed, this time. He had learned that on the lake. He did not shove his qi into her like a man emptying a sack into a well; he *let* it go, a thread at a time, and the threads ran down the bond and into her and her reversed meridians took each one and turned it over, down and in, the way the tide turns a stone, until it lay in her as quietly as if it had always been hers. He followed it with his nose to make sure it settled. It did. It settled the way water settles in a bowl.

*Better,* thought Romeo. *Much better.* And then, because there was no qi left in him at all and his channels were suddenly as empty as a drum, the salt bag on his back stopped being a comfort and became merely heavy, and the cold came up through the blanket, and he shivered. That was not acting either.

The collector's bare hand came down toward him.

It did not touch. It stopped a finger's width above the red saddle of his back, and stayed there, palm down, and Romeo felt it the way he had felt the stranger standing in the shaft of the den: not as cold, exactly, but as a place where warmth was not allowed to go. All the heat of the salt bag fled sideways from it. The fur along his spine lay down flat of its own accord.

The hand moved, very slowly, up the length of him. Over the hips, over the white band. Over the saddle. Toward the shoulders.

Toward the back of his neck.

The flinch began in him before he could stop it, the old one, the violent one, the one that came whenever anything closed round his neck from above. He felt his muscles bunch for it. He felt his ruined back get ready to throw itself sideways off the bench, and he knew, with perfect clarity, that if he did that his spine would go and he would scream, and an ill unawakened dog might scream, but it would not move like *that*.

Under the floor of the world, the thread tied with his name hummed.

Not a lift. Not a pull. It sang, very faintly, the way a bowl sings when you run a wet finger round its rim, and the note of it came from straight above him: from the bare white hand.

"Don't," said Ling Mei. "Don't touch him."

She did not say it loudly. She said it the way she said *right*: flat, and certain, and with no room in it for anybody to argue. Her hand was still on his scruff. Her other hand, the bound one, the crooked one, had come up and was lying across the back of his neck, between him and the collector, a bandage and five fingers and nothing else.

"His back," she said. "You'll hurt him."

The bare hand stopped.

For a long moment nothing in the tack room moved. The brazier ticked. Somewhere out in the yard a chain dragged on stone and stopped. Romeo lay under Ling Mei's two hands and the collector's one, and did not flinch, and did not breathe, and smelled, from very close, the silver line under the skin of that fourth finger. It was old silver. Older than the stone's band. Older than the pin. It had the dull, close, worn smell of a ring that has been on the same finger for so long that the finger has grown round it.

And for the space of one heartbeat, right at the edge of the nothing, he caught something else. Not a smell, quite. The ghost of one, the way you can sometimes smell rain in a dry wind and know it has fallen somewhere far away. It was very faint and very sweet.

Peach.

Then the collector drew his hand back, and the nothing went with it, and the heat of the salt bag came flooding back into the place where it had been.

"Of course," he said pleasantly. "Forgive me. I forget how they feel about hands, the small ones." He drew the grey glove back on, finger by finger, smoothing the kid leather down over the knuckles with his right hand until the silver line was hidden and the nothing was hidden and he was a slight, straight man in a grey coat again. "I gave this one away a long time ago. It's quite usual, in my work."

Nobody asked to whom.

"Elder Fang's room, then," said the collector. "That's sensible. It's very warm, and very high up, and the Elder never sleeps." The mask tilted down at Romeo once more. "Keep him there till light. Don't take him home tonight, Miss Ling. I'm told your hut is occupied."

He went out under the lintel. At the door he paused, the way he had paused after knocking, as if giving them time.

"Good dog," he said, and closed it softly behind him.

---

It was some while before anybody spoke. Smoke came out of the physician's collar first, an inch at a time, and looked at the door, and then at Romeo.

⟨What was that,⟩ said Smoke. It was not quite a question. ⟨What was that, what was that.⟩

⟨A hand,⟩ said Romeo. ⟨Somebody's.⟩

He brought his qi home. That went better too: he did not have to coax it out of her thread by thread as he had in the hut, like someone taking hot stones out of a bed. It came back to his nose when he called it, turned over and quiet, the way a good dog comes back to a whistle. Ling Mei let her breath out through her teeth.

⟨Did it hurt?⟩ he asked her.

"Less," she whispered. "It went in like it knew the way." She did not take her hands off him. "He knew. About my hut. He *knew*."

⟨He knew something was in it,⟩ said Romeo. ⟨He did not say it was you.⟩

"That's worse."

⟨Yes.⟩

He did not tell her about the peach. He did not know how. Three thousand one hundred and twelve years ago a spirit peach had fallen out of a tree in a poor man's yard and a young dog had eaten it because it was there, and that was the first thing he had ever been, before Romeo, before the river. He had never in all the years since smelled another like it. He had not been sure, until a heartbeat ago, that there had ever been another.

*Later,* he told himself. *Sit.* In Shen's voice, with the laugh at the end. It helped a little.

---

The physician carried him across the yard because the old man's arms were steadier than Ling Mei's tonight, and she walked close at his elbow with one hand on Romeo's ear.

The ring of posts stood round the stone in the last of the light. Twenty-six of them, new wood, pale against the snow, and at the foot of each one a wolf lay chained with ordinary iron, nose on paws, not howling. Grey Nine. Iron-Jaw. Third Cloud. Ashfoot. Others whose names he had only ever smelled. Between the posts, laid out on the trampled snow like nets on a beach, coil after coil of silver chain lay waiting, making no sound at all.

The lamp burned by the stone. Ah-Shu stood beside it in his mask, so still that the snow had begun to settle on his shoulders again, with his right hand pushed deep into his belt.

As they passed he did not look at them. But the mask turned, very slightly, a hand's width, toward the gap between the last two storehouses, where the black pines began and the path went up to the spring. Then it turned back.

*Yes,* thought Romeo. *I know where it is. I know who is not sitting at the top of it.*

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the thing he had been turning over since the woodpile. The room in the rock was open tonight, and only tonight, and its keeper was sitting on a floorboard at the far end of the outer lane with its knees drawn up, wearing Ling Mei's voice, waiting for a dog who could not climb.

*Let it keep the board warm.*

They were at the foot of the Beast Hall steps when the sound came. It was very small. It was the sound a chain makes when the weight goes out of it all at once: a slither, and a soft iron *chink* in the snow.

Romeo turned his head on the physician's arm.

At the seventh post, the chain lay slack. The collar at the end of it was still closed, still locked, lying in a perfect round in the snow with nothing inside it, and the snow inside the round was smooth and warm-looking and printed, faintly, with the shape of a wolf that had been lying there with its nose on its paws.

Grey Nine had not made a sound.

None of the others did either. They lay at their posts and did not lift their heads. And high above the yard, in the warm upper room of the Beast Hall, behind the old wavy glass of Elder Fang's window, Romeo heard Brow begin, very low, a noise he had never heard a bound wolf make: the long, thin whimper of a pup in the dark, that cannot find its mother.

# **Chapter 24: A Coat Name**

The beast-physician had folded himself into the den the way a heron folds itself into a teapot: knees, elbows, a long grey beard, and a great deal of patience. He sat with his back against the curved wall of the round chamber and his feet in the mouth of the seam tunnel, and when Ling Mei lifted the third floorboard and the grey light fell in on him, he did not startle. He only opened his one good eye.  
"Ah," he said, in his voice like a door that needs oil. "The dog that ran off."  
Romeo looked down the shaft. It was the height of a tall man, and he had dug it himself, and he had gone down it a hundred times headfirst with his ears flying. He looked at it now and his back said, very quietly and very clearly, *No.*  
Ling Mei saw him look. She knelt, took hold of the loose hemp harness behind his shoulders, and lowered him hand over hand into the dark like a bucket into a well.  
*I am not luggage,* he told her, revolving gently.  
"You're twenty-four buckets," said Ling Mei. "Hold still."  
He landed on the earth floor beside the old man's knee. Smoke flowed down the wall after him, ran once round the chamber as though checking that nothing had been stolen while he was up top, and settled across the physician's shoulders like a scarf.  
Romeo did not look at either of them. He walked, stiff as a wooden toy, to the shallow trough he had scraped in the floor where the earth qi came up thickest, and lay down in it, and let the cool slow strength of the mountain soak up into his belly. Something in his spine unclenched by the width of a hair. It was the best thing that had happened to him in two days.  
Then he lifted his head and gave the old man the look a dachshund gives a stranger sitting in his burrow.  
---

Above them Ling Mei lit the charcoal bowl and set it on the boards at the edge of the opening, so that a little dull red light came down the shaft. She sat with her legs dangling into the hole and her coat pulled round her, and the end of Romeo's cord still wrapped twice around her wrist, as though she had forgotten it was there.  
"He can't hear you," she said to Romeo. "Can he?"  
*No. Only you.* Romeo turned his eyes to the stoat. *And this one, who has been in my den all day with his feet on my things.*  
*I'll tell him,* said Smoke cheerfully. *I'll tell him all of it. I'll do the voices.*  
*Tell him only what I say.*  
*Where's the fun in that.* The stoat turned his head and touched his nose to the old man's ear, and the physician's mouth twitched at the corner.  
"He says," the physician reported gravely, "that you are a wet sock that has been dragged through a pine tree." He paused. "I think that may be Smoke. Is it Smoke?"  
"That's mine, actually," said Ling Mei. "I said it first."  
The physician looked up the shaft at her for a long moment, with his one eye. Then he nodded slowly, as if she had answered a question he hadn't asked.  
"Then I'll start," he said, "and the dog can stop me where I'm wrong."  
---

"Forty years ago," said the physician, "I was a junior doctor at Clear Bell Pavilion on the third tier. A good sect. Rich. They kept a beast yard with two hundred bound animals in it, and I had one of my own: a brindle hound bitch with a white sock on one foot. Her name was Lantern."  
He said the name very carefully, the way a man sets down a cup that is too full.  
"One spring a cart came up the road with a stone on it. Waist-high. Grey. Shaped like a tooth, with a band of silver round it. The Pavilion's beast-master said the Court had sent it to assess the yard. Every beast was led up and touched to it and named, and the stone said the name back, and everyone thought it a fine thing." He rubbed his thumb along the knuckles of his other hand. "Nothing happened for a long time. Then the beasts started going quiet. Not sick. They ate. They obeyed. They simply stopped turning their heads when you called them. Stopped dreaming. A beast that dreams twitches; you learn that, doing my work. They stopped twitching."  
Romeo, in his trough, had gone very still.  
"Nine months after the stone came, at the dark of the moon, the first one went. A crane. In the morning there was the chain, still locked to the post, and the collar, still locked, and no crane." The physician's beard moved as he swallowed. "After that, one every dark of the moon. Lantern went in the autumn. The week before, I called her across the yard and she did not turn her head. She looked at me afterwards, when I came and stood in front of her. She knew me. She simply didn't know she was the one I was calling."  
Ling Mei had both hands over her mouth.  
"Then men in grey coats came, and put the stone back on its cart, and took it up the road. Not down. Up." The physician looked at the earth wall as if he could see through it. "I left the Pavilion. I came down to the first tier, where nobody would ever spend a stone like that on a yard of outer-sect wolves. I was wrong about that. A month ago the cart came through our gate."  
---

*Ask him,* said Romeo to Smoke, *why he kept his mouth shut for a month.*  
Smoke asked, more or less. The physician did not seem offended.  
"I didn't," he said. "I went to Elder Fang the same day. I told him everything I've told you." He was quiet for a moment. "He let me finish. Then he said, *I know what it is.* He said, *Say nothing to anyone. Keep your stoat away from it.* And he sent me out."  
Romeo's nose went through the air in the den, slowly, the way a hand goes through a drawer in the dark. Bitter herbs. Old paper. Charcoal from above. Stoat. And the old man himself: thin sweat, old grief worn smooth, a sour tang of shame, and under everything a plain, dry steadiness that did not change when he spoke of Fang.  
No lie. Not a grain of one.  
*He knew,* Romeo said to Ling Mei. *Fang knew what it was, and let it into his yard, and stood beside it while his own wolves were named to it.*  
"Then why..." Ling Mei began, and stopped, because none of them knew why.  
"So I have kept my stoat away from it," said the physician. "And I have watched the wolves stop dreaming. Grey Nine first. Then Iron-Jaw. And I have done nothing, because I am an old man with one eye and a stoat, and because the elder told me to." He looked at Romeo directly. "And then a dog came into my kennels who smelled of earth qi and talked to my stoat, and walked away from that stone after it had read him, still twitching in his sleep. I thought: I will tell this one. Whatever he is."  
*Tell him,* said Romeo, *that I am old.*  
Smoke told him. The physician laughed, once, a creak like the door finally opening.  
"Yes," he said. "I rather thought so."  
---

"There's one more thing," said the physician, "and it is the only useful thing I have."  
He reached up and laid two fingers, very gently, on the stoat's back.  
"His name isn't Smoke."  
Smoke's whiskers flattened. For once he did not say anything clever.  
"Smoke is a coat," said the physician. "A thing I call him across a yard. His real name he told me once, the day we bound, and I have never said it aloud since. Not to him, not to anyone, not in my sleep, as far as I know. He knows it. He keeps it behind his teeth." The old man's fingers moved along the narrow back. "At the Pavilion, I worked it out too late. The stone can't take a name out of the air. It takes the one a beast answers to, when that name is said over it while it touches the silver. That's the edge it bites. Then it works inward from there, a little at a time, the way water works into a crack. Every time someone calls the beast by that name, the crack opens a little wider."  
Romeo lay in the trough with the earth qi coming up through his belly, and thought about the yard. About the steward's brush scratching. About Fang's ledger: *Name: Romeo.* About the stone saying it back in that light, pleasant voice.  
The stone had bitten *Romeo.* It had bitten Shen's name, and the river, and the hat in the brown water, because those were the edges that name was made of.  
It had not bitten what was underneath.  
Three thousand years ago a dog with no name had eaten a fallen peach and woken up knowing he was someone, and the first thing he had done with that knowledge, before any man ever called him anything, was give himself a name. He had not said it aloud since. It was still there, under Romeo, like a stone under a river. He could feel it now, the way you feel a tooth with your tongue.  
Whole. Unbitten.  
*Ling Mei,* he said.  
No answer. He looked up the shaft. She had put her head down on her knees beside the charcoal bowl and fallen asleep sitting up, with the cord still wrapped around her wrist and her mouth a little open. The red light lay along her cheek.  
---

So it was Romeo who decided, alone, what the old man would get in return.  
He gave him the pin. The silver pin wedged in the seam below the black-pine spring, and the still escort carrying name-edges up to it on his bare palm in the dark, and the fact that the names, once they reached it, went on uphill. He gave him that because the old man had earned it, and because a man who knew where the names went might, one day, stand at the right door.  
He did not give him Bao. Bao was not his to give. He did not give him the voice in the slot, or the hat, or the river. Those he kept behind his teeth.  
The physician listened to Smoke relay it, and went grey.  
"Uphill," he said. "Uphill to what?"  
*Tell him,* said Romeo, *that I'm going to find out.*  
The old man unfolded himself, joint by joint, toward the back-door tunnel; he had come in that way and would have to go out on his knees through the ditch reeds like a much younger and more foolish person. At the mouth of it he stopped.  
"At the Pavilion," he said, without turning round, "it was nine months from the stone to the first one gone. Here it has been one month, and they are already quiet." He let that sit in the dark. "Whoever is at the other end of your pin is hungry, dog. Or in a hurry."  
Then he was gone, with Smoke riding his shoulders and saying something rude about the reeds.  
---

Romeo did not mean to sleep. He meant to lie in the trough until he had worked out what Fang Wuji knew, and why a man who said *Things that are mine, I keep* would let a stone into his yard that took them.  
He slept before he had finished the first thought.  
He slept the way old dogs sleep, all at once and completely, flat on his side with his legs out and his ears spread on the earth like two dropped gloves. The earth qi came up into him slow and cool and patient. The last of Bao's water qi, still lodged in his channels like silt after a flood, began to move with it. Somewhere in the night his paws twitched, running through a dream of a river, and he did not wake.

He woke a little after midnight, warm for the first time in two days, and lay with his eyes open in the dark, reading that last line over again. The System had not given him a recommendation in a thousand years.  
Then, overhead, someone knocked on the door of the hut.  
Three knocks. Then two.  
Ling Mei did not stir. And Romeo, who knew exactly who in the world used that knock, and knew that one of them was asleep under a lake in the second tier and the other had been gone for a very long time, lay rigid in the earth with every hair on his long body standing up, and did not breathe.

# **Chapter 20: Three Knocks, Then Two**

There is a sensible thing to do when you are wet to the skin, most of two nights short of sleep, two levels into a cultivation that once reached the Ninth Gate, and your tail has just pointed up a mountain after a man in a mask. The sensible thing is to go home, climb into a hole under a sleeping girl, and be cross with your tail in the morning.  
Romeo lay in the mouth of the drain and thought about this for the length of three breaths.  
*She'll find the boot,* he thought. *She'll know I went out. She won't know I went up.*  
The still escort had reached the last two storehouses. In a moment the dark between them would have him.  
*One step,* said Old Man Shen, from very far away, the way he always did when Romeo least wanted to hear him. *And then the next one.*  
Romeo came out of the drain.  
---

The trouble with following a man through snow is that the man is tall.  
The escort walked the way big men walk in drifts, lifting each boot high and putting it down straight, and he left behind him a line of holes, each as deep as a dachshund's shoulder and a long stride apart. Romeo reached the first one, looked down into it, and understood at once that this was going to be undignified.  
He could not walk between the holes; the snow there came up over his back. He could not walk in them; they were too far apart. So he went from hole to hole the only way his body allowed, a scrabble and a lunge and a short flat leap, landing in each boot-print with his front half in it and his back half still arriving, like a sausage being fed into a series of buckets. Twice he misjudged and went in nose-first. Once his hind legs failed to follow at all, and he hung there over the lip with his long back bowed and aching and his ears in the snow, until he could get one paw under himself and heave.  
*Three thousand years,* he thought, spitting snow. *I have walked the Thousand-Li Ice Road in a single night. I have crossed the Weeping River on the back of its own dragon, which did not want me there.* He landed in the next hole. *And now I am losing an argument with a man's feet.*  
But the holes were good for one thing. They were deep enough to hide him. Each time he dropped into one he was gone from the world, and each time he came up he could put his nose over the rim and read the slope ahead before he moved again.  
Lacquer. Leather. Horse. The sweat under heavy cloth, cold now, gone sour with the climb. Silver, as always. And under it, faint as a word said in another room, the river.  
The escort did not look back once. That was what frightened Romeo most. A man who does not look back on a dark slope is either very foolish or very sure he is alone, and this one did not smell foolish.  
---

Beyond the storehouses the ground rose steeply toward the black pines. The trees stood in a dark wall along the ridge with their branches weighed down to the snow, and around the foot of every trunk the snow had not fallen so thickly, so that each pine stood in a hollow of its own, a deep bowl of needles and shadow under a roof of white boughs.  
Romeo knew tree wells. Every creature that has ever lived below a pine forest in winter knows tree wells, mostly because something it was fond of fell into one and never came out. He went into the nearest on his belly, under the low branches, and down the inside of the bowl until his paws found dry needles and the hard roots of the pine.  
It was almost warm in there. It smelled of resin and old mice. He could have slept for a hundred years.  
He put his nose to the gap between two boughs instead and looked out.  
Thirty paces up the slope the spring came out of the rock. It did not freeze; it never had. It ran steaming down a short black channel in the stone, and at the bottom of the channel, under a hand's depth of moving water, lay the silver pin, wedged in the seam where Romeo had found it. Beside it, a hairline crack ran back into the hillside, too narrow for a finger and wide enough for a smell. Silk. Lamp smoke. A dry passage that something walked.  
The escort knelt beside the channel.  
He knelt carefully, the way a man kneels at an altar he does not quite believe in. He looked once along the ridge, left and right. Then he drew off his right glove, finger by finger, and laid it on the stone beside him, and put his bare hand down into the steaming water until his palm rested flat on the pin.  
Romeo breathed in.  
The hand, now that the glove was off it, smelled of a great many things at once. Clean skin. Old calluses from a spear shaft. And over all of it, fine as flour on a baker's fingers, a dusting of names. Not whole names. Edges of them. Grey Nine's, a little. Iron-Jaw's. A wolf he did not know. And the river, a golden afternoon, one step further off than it should have been.  
*He gathered them from the band,* Romeo thought. *Every time he put his hand on the stone.*  
And now he was touching the pin with that hand, and the names were moving.  
Romeo reached for the new thing the night had taught him, the lean, the shape a scent makes where it is not. He found it at once. Between the bare palm and the silver there was a draught, a flow, unmistakable. Names were passing between the man and the pin.  
He could not tell which way.  
He tried. He pushed the Nine Scents channel until his small ember of qi burned in his chest like a swallowed coal, and the lean stayed exactly where it was, a blur of motion between two things a hand's width apart. Into the pin, or out of it. Feeding it, or robbing it. At this distance, with these meridians, his nose could tell him only that something was being carried, and not where.

*I know,* Romeo told it, with some bitterness.  
The escort kept his hand on the pin for the space of perhaps twenty breaths. Then he took it out of the water, shook it once, and did not put the glove back on. He sat on his heels and held the wet hand against his chest, under the coat, the way a man holds a thing he does not want to drop.  
Then the tapping started.  
---

It came from the crack. Very faint, very regular, the sound of something hard touching stone, a long way back in the hill. Tap. A pause. Tap. Coming nearer.  
Romeo's fur stood up from the back of his neck to the white tip of his tail.  
The escort heard it too. And he did a thing Romeo did not expect at all.  
He was afraid.  
It came off him all at once, through the cold sweat and the silver, the sharp bright reek of a man who has heard a sound he was hoping not to hear. He snatched up his glove. He got to his feet with no care at all for the snow. He looked round him once, wildly, for somewhere to go, and then he came down the slope in three long sliding strides and threw himself flat behind the nearest cover.  
The nearest cover was Romeo's pine.  
The boughs above the well shook and dropped their snow. A great dark shape landed on the lip of the bowl, close enough that Romeo felt the warmth of it through the branches, and lay there breathing hard with its mouthless lacquer face turned uphill toward the spring.  
Romeo did not move. He did not breathe. He lay on the needles with his chin on a root and his ears flat and his whole long body pressed into the ground, as he had pressed it into ten thousand earths in three thousand years, and he begged every god he had ever bitten for the man not to look down.  
The tapping reached the spring.  
It stopped there, just inside the crack, the way a walker stops at a window. Romeo could smell it now. Silk. Lamp smoke. Silver. And nothing else: no sweat, no breath, no intent, nothing that a living thing ought to smell of. The stranger. Standing a hand's width inside the hill, with only a crack between it and the pin the escort had just been touching.  
It tapped the pin. Once.  
Somewhere above Romeo the escort made a very small sound, and then made none.  
And it was at that moment, with death or worse a few paces up the slope, that Romeo's nose, which had been running since the drain and had been patient about it for hours, decided that it had been patient enough.  
He felt it coming from a long way off. A tickle, high up behind the eyes, where resin and cold and old mice had been gathering. He knew this feeling. The last time he had felt it, it had ended on the bare hand of a Hound-Catcher.  
*No,* he told it.  
The tickle grew.  
*Sit,* he told it, and it was the hardest word he knew, and he had never once hated it so little. *Sit. Stay.*  
He pushed his muzzle down into the dry needles until his nostrils were stopped with them, and held it there, and held it, while the whole of his small body shook with the effort, and his eyes watered, and the scar across his muzzle burned. Inside the hill, the stranger stood still. Above him, the escort lay still. Between them, in a hole under a tree, a small red-and-white dog fought the greatest battle of his second life against his own face.  
The tapping began again. Moving away. Uphill, deeper into the hill, along the seam. Tap. A pause. Tap. Fainter. Gone.  
Romeo let out one tiny, strangled breath into the pine needles, which was not a sneeze, and which no one would ever be able to prove was a sneeze.  
---

The escort got up slowly.  
He stood on the lip of the tree well, brushing snow from his coat, and pulled his glove back on over the wet hand. Then he looked down.  
Romeo looked up.  
The lacquer mask had no mouth. Its eye-slits were black. Between the branches, for a long moment, a Hound-Catcher's escort and an unbound, awakened, three-thousand-year-old dog regarded each other in the dark, and Romeo did not know whether he was about to feel a silver chain go round his neck from above.  
His neck flinched. He let it.  
The escort crouched. He reached in through the boughs. His gloved hand stopped a finger's width from Romeo's ear, and Romeo smelled on it, very close, the river.  
"Go home, dog," the man said. His voice was low and hoarse, a soldier's voice, nothing like the collector's. "Go home and stay there."  
He took his hand away. He stood up and went back down the slope toward the sect, not looking back, walking in his own boot-holes.  
Romeo lay under the pine and smelled him go. Fear, still, and silver, and the river going away with him down the hill.  
*That,* he thought, *was either a kindness or a warning. Or a man telling a dog to go home, which is what men say to dogs.* He could not smell a lie in it. He could not smell much of anything in it, except that the man had meant it.  
---

He should have gone home. He went to the crack.  
The spring steamed. The pin lay in its seam under the moving water, wedged, silver, exactly as it had been. He put his nose to the crack beside it and breathed in the dry passage: stone, old dust, silk, lamp smoke, and very far back, so faint he might have imagined it, cold lake water and the slow mineral smell of something ten thousand years old.  
The passage went to the lake. Of course it did. It went to Bao.  
Romeo laid his chest against the stone the way he had once barked through it, and listened, not with his ears but with the whole length of himself, the way a badger-hound listens to the earth before he goes into it.  
Nothing. Water. His own heart.  
Then, from somewhere so far up the hill that it came to him less as a sound than as a shiver in the rock, a knocking.  
Three knocks.  
Then two.  
Romeo went entirely still. That was Bao's code. *Three knocks, then two,* the old turtle had told him. *And if it's the end of the world, knock louder.* It was a code for waking Bao. It was meant to be knocked from outside.  
This was coming from inside.  
The rock was quiet for the space of a breath. Then the knocking came again, and this time it was louder.

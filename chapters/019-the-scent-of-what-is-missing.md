# **Chapter 19: The Scent of What Is Missing**

Ling Mei was asleep when he came up the shaft.

She had not meant to be. She was sitting on the floor with her back against the sleeping platform and the blanket round her shoulders, exactly where a person sits when she intends to stay awake until a dog comes back, and her head had gone over sideways onto her own good arm. The brazier had sunk to a red eye. Her right hand, for once, was perfectly still.

Romeo stood with his front paws on the lip of the third floorboard and looked at her for some time.

*She'll be furious,* he thought. *She'll call me a sock. Possibly an old boot.*

He thought about waking her. He thought about the drain to the kennels, which was a dog's width and not a girl's, and about the two masks standing in the snow by the stone, and about the fact that this was the first time in three days she had slept without her hand shaking. Then he did what he had done, one way or another, for every person he had ever loved, which was to decide for her and feel bad about it later.

He went to the doorway and dragged her boot across it with his teeth, so that she could not get up in the dark without falling over it and knowing he had gone out. It was not a note. It was the best a dog could do.

Then he went back down the hole.

---

The back-door tunnel ran low and damp to the ditch, and he went along it the way water goes along a pipe, belly to the earth, ears flat, the long sausage of him folding round the one bend like a rope round a post. At the ditch end the reeds were frozen stiff and rattled if you touched them. He did not touch them. He lay in the mouth of the tunnel with only his nose out and read the night.

Snow, old and new. A cat, two days stale. The kennels downhill, a great warm reek of wolf and straw and old meat. And to the left, across the outer yard, the stone.

He could smell it from here. That was new. This morning it had smelled like any third-tier rock with a silver band round it, hoarded names under the mineral. Tonight it smelled like a mouth after eating. And beside it, the two escorts: lacquer, leather, horse, cold sweat under heavy cloth, and silver. Always silver. One of them was shifting from foot to foot. The other was not moving at all.

*The still one is the dangerous one,* thought Romeo, who had stood a great many night watches in his time and knew the difference between a guard who is bored and a guard who is listening.

The ditch ran downhill along the bottom of the yard, below the line of their sight if they kept their eyes on the stone. He went along it in short runs, stopping in every place where the drifted snow was deep enough to hide a small red dog lying flat, and in the deep places he did not walk so much as plough, chest-first, with his short legs paddling behind. It was slow. It was cold.

The bored mask coughed. Romeo froze with one forepaw lifted, the way badger-hounds have frozen at the mouths of setts since long before anyone thought to keep records, with a badger's breath on the wind.

Nothing came. He went on.

The drain was where the ditch met the kennel wall: a square stone mouth, half full of ice, with a smell coming out of it that would have turned a man's stomach and only made Romeo feel mildly at home. He put his head in. The ice had a crust on top and a channel of open dark beneath it where the kennel's warmth had melted it from below.

*Like a sausage down a drain,* he thought, with resignation, and went.

---

He came up through a grating in the floor of the end stall, which had been propped open with a horse's shinbone, and found himself nose to nose with the stoat.

⟨You're late,⟩ said Smoke.

⟨I am exactly on time,⟩ said Romeo, dripping. ⟨The hour of the rat is long. Like me.⟩

Smoke's whiskers did something that might have been a stoat's opinion of jokes. Then it turned and flowed away over the straw, and Romeo, after one hard shake that sent drain water up the stall walls, followed.

The kennels of the Azure Wolf Sect were a long low barn of black timber, with stalls down both sides and a walkway of packed earth between. A single lamp burned at the far end, turned down to almost nothing. In its light the wolves lay in their straw like grey boulders: Iron-Jaw, Third Cloud, Ashfoot, Grey Nine with his chin on the stall door. Every one of them lifted its head as Romeo passed. None of them made a sound. Romeo could smell the bindings running out of them, silver threads going up through the roof toward the senior halls where their masters slept, and he could smell, in each of them, the place where the stone had drunk.

It was not a wound. It was more like a room where a picture has been taken down from the wall, and the paint behind it is a different colour.

The beast-physician was sitting on an upturned bucket under the lamp.

Up close he was older than Romeo had thought. His face had fallen in on itself like a winter apple. One eye squinted shut; the other was clear and brown and not stupid at all. He had a wooden box on his knees.

"The elder," he said aloud, in a voice like a door that needs oil, "has given orders that the disciple's dog is to be kept out of the kennels." He considered Romeo, dripping on his earth floor. "He said nothing about the drain."

Romeo sat. He did not speak. He had spoken mind-to-mind to no human in this body but Ling Mei, and he did not mean to start tonight. He tilted his head, which was the most a dog is obliged to say to anyone.

The physician gave a small dry wheeze that was probably a laugh. "No. I wouldn't either." He opened the box.

Inside, on a fold of oiled paper, was a little heap of grey grit.

"From the foot of it," said the physician. "The day it came. I scraped it with a hoof knife while the deacons were arguing about where to put it." He held the box down, and Romeo put his nose to it, carefully, the way you put your nose to a thing that might bite.

Rock. Cold. A thread of silver in it, very fine, from the band. And nothing else.

Nothing. Not a single name. Not a ghost of one. The stone, from a hand's width away, reeked of hoarded names, and the grit cut from its foot smelled of no one at all.

*It doesn't keep them,* Romeo thought slowly. *It's a mouth. Not a belly.*

⟨That's what he thinks,⟩ said Smoke from the straw. ⟨He has thought it for a month. He had no way to know where it swallows to.⟩ The bright eyes turned to Romeo. ⟨He hoped you might.⟩

---

There is a thing a nose can do that even most hounds never learn, because most hounds are not three thousand years old and have never needed it. It is not following a scent. Any puppy can follow a scent. It is following the shape that a scent leaves when it is gone.

Old Man Shen had taught it to him without knowing he was teaching anything. They had come home once to find the hut's one copper pot missing, and Romeo, very young and very proud, had run in circles looking for the smell of a thief, and found none. *Don't look for what's there,* the old man had said, sitting down on the step. *Look for the pot. Where's the smell of the pot not, that it ought to be?* And Romeo had found the clean ring in the dust on the shelf, and the line of nothing going from it to the window, and a crow's nest in the plum tree with a copper pot in it.

He went to Grey Nine's stall.

The old wolf watched him come and did not lift a lip. Romeo put his nose through the bars, close to the grey ruff, to the place where the picture had come down off the wall. He breathed in slowly through the Nine Scents channel, all the way down to the small hot ember of his cultivation, and he did not look for Grey Nine's name. He looked for where it was not.

And there it was. Not a smell. A lean. The way a candle flame leans toward a crack in a shutter. Grey Nine's missing name had a direction, the way the empty ring on Shen's shelf had a direction, and it went down through the straw and the packed earth and away, under the kennel wall, uphill.

He went to Iron-Jaw. Uphill. Third Cloud. Uphill. Ashfoot, who growled at him very softly and then let him do it anyway. Uphill, all of them, every lean in the barn tilting the same way like grass in a current, toward the north side of the sect, where the ground rose toward the black pines.

Toward the spring. Toward the seam. Toward a wedged silver pin and the hairline crack beside it that opened onto a dry passage where something tall and narrow walked, tapping.

Romeo stood very still in the walkway. Then, because he had to know, he did the thing he did not want to do, and turned his nose on himself. Down into his own chest, to the place where a river afternoon lived, to the smell of the water that was one step further off than it had been this morning.

It leaned uphill too.

⟨The pins,⟩ Romeo said. He did not realise he had said it into the stoat's head until Smoke flinched. ⟨The names don't stay in the stone. They go into the ground and run along the seam to the pins. The same pins that hold Bao.⟩

⟨Pins?⟩ said Smoke. ⟨Who is Bao?⟩

Romeo did not answer. He was thinking of a ten-thousand-year-old turtle sitting alone under a lake with twenty-four silver threads in him, and of the new moon, six nights off, when a sweatless thing would come to tie the threads again. And he was wondering, for the first time, what it was that the threads were tied *with*.

---

The fox was in the last stall on the left.

Her cage had been set down in the straw with the door still locked, and someone, the physician by the smell of it, had pushed a dish of water and a strip of boiled liver through the bars. The liver had not been touched. The vixen lay curled with her nose under her thin tail, and she was so still and so nameless that Romeo, passing, would have taken her for a fur on a peg if he had not known.

The shaving at his throat moved.

He felt it through the lunch cloth, a tiny tug, like a fish touching a line. It had pointed at this cage every time it came near. It pointed now, so hard he had to brace his short legs against it, not at the fox but at the lock: a plain iron lock with a silver hasp through it, a hasp of the same pale, clean, soundless silver as the pin under the black-pine spring.

Romeo put his nose to the fox's ruff, the way he had to the wolves'. He looked for the lean.

There was none.

Every other beast in this barn had a draught going out of it, uphill, into the earth. The fox had nothing going anywhere. Her name was not here, but it was not running away along the seam either. It was being held. He could even tell where: a long, cold, patient knot of it, twenty paces off through two walls, in the senior hall, lying at an elder's knee.

*Brow,* he thought. *Brow has it. Brow is holding it so the pins can't.*

Which meant that the fox was the one beast in the Azure Wolf Sect whose name had not been eaten. And the collector had brought her here, and set her down at the foot of the steps where Brow could smell her, and said *She ought to finish in them,* and stayed six nights to see what would come.

The vixen opened one eye. It was gold and very young and it looked straight at the blue cloth round his neck.

"That's enough," said the physician softly from his bucket. "Guard changes soon. You'll want to be under the snow when it does."

---

He went back by the drain, faster than he had come, and lay in the mouth of it with the ice at his chest and his nose just out over the ditch.

The guard was changing. Two new masks came across the yard from the guest hall, snow squeaking under their boots, and stopped by the stone. A few words passed, too low to hear. The bored one stamped his feet and went off toward the guest hall and his bed.

The still one did not.

The still one stood a moment by the stone with one gloved hand flat on its silver band, the way the physician had touched it after every beast. Then he took the hand away and walked, not toward the guest hall, but north, across the far corner of the yard and out between the last two storehouses onto the slope, where the ground rose into the dark toward the black pines and the spring.

The wind was coming downhill. It brought Romeo lacquer, and horse, and cold. It brought him silver. And under the silver, very faint, a smell that had no business on any man's glove: an old river in a golden afternoon, one step further off than it should have been.

His tail moved before he had decided anything.

It pointed uphill.

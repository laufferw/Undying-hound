# **Chapter 6: Rough Water**

The bitterroot let go of him a little after the hour of the goat, the way a cold snap lets go of a pond: all at once, with a long creak somewhere deep down, and then the water underneath was moving again.

Romeo felt his qi stir. Felt the small sea he had gathered over three days turn over in its bed like a sleeper, sluggish and sour-tasting, and then settle. His mind-voice came back clean. He tested it on the nearest available listener.

*That,* he told the floor of the hut, *was a very stupid plan, and it worked, and I would like it noted that I do not intend to do it again.*

The floor did not answer. Ling Mei had gone to her afternoon chores, hauling water for the outer kitchens with arms that ached to the shoulder, and had left him behind with a crust, a strict instruction not to be seen, and a look that said she knew perfectly well what he would do the moment she was gone.

He went down the hole.

---

The trouble with the earth-qi vein was that it was in the wrong place.

It ran along the bottom of his round chamber, hair-thin, following the rainwater seam down from somewhere up the hill: a cool, slow thread of earth energy no wider than a whisker, which he had found on his first night and had been quietly sleeping on ever since. It was a fine thing for a dog. A dog could curl up on it and let it seep into his belly and gather at its own pace. But Ling Mei was not a dog. She was a girl of nineteen with a girl's shoulders, and his den had been dug by a dachshund for a dachshund, and the closest she could get to the vein was to lie on the floor of the hut with her arm down the entrance shaft as far as it would go.

Which stopped a full forearm's length short of the bend.

So Romeo dug.

He dug the way his body had been made to dig, before anyone had made it immortal or clever or anything else: front paws going like a loom, hind legs braced, long back curved into the work, dirt flying out behind him in a steady brown fan. Somewhere far back in the dark of him, a hundred generations of small stubborn dogs who had been sent down holes after badgers sat up and said, as one, *Yes. This. Finally something sensible.*

He did not dig blindly. He followed the seam with his nose. The rainwater had worn its own soft path through the clay, and where it went, the earth qi went with it, and where he scraped the clay away along that path, the thread of qi spread a little wider in the open channel, the way water spreads when you lift a stone out of a stream.

It took him the whole afternoon. By the end, he had cut a shallow trough from the chamber floor up under the bend, and the vein lay along the bottom of it like a silver thread in a brown sleeve, its tail end reaching to the foot of the entrance shaft. He had also, without noticing, sent a small avalanche of dirt up through the gap by the third floorboard and across the floor of the hut, where it had found the rice pot.

Ling Mei came home at dusk, looked at the floor, looked at the rice pot, and said nothing for a long moment.

Then she said, "There is *dirt* in my *dinner*."

*There is a great deal of dirt in a great many places,* Romeo agreed, emerging from the hole with his whole front half the colour of the mountain and his ears inside out. *It is the nature of improvements.*

---

She ate the rice anyway. Romeo ate his crust beside her and watched her hands. The left one would not close all the way around the bowl. The right had a tremor in it that came and went like a bird landing and lifting off a branch.

He had smelled the tide in her all day, through the floor, through the walls, even down the hole: a hot, crowded, salt smell, the smell of a river pushing at banks it had been running against the wrong way for nineteen years. Two percent was nothing. And every night that he turned it, the turning hurt more, because a river that has begun to turn pushes harder against the banks that are left.

"Will tonight be bad?" she asked, not looking at him.

*Tonight will be different,* he said. *Lie on the floor. On your front. Put your arm down the hole.*

She looked at the hole, and then at him, and then at the hole again.

"That is the least dignified thing anyone has ever asked me to do."

*I spent last night in a sewer under a wolf,* said Romeo. *Dignity is a luxury of the well-cultivated.*

---

When Romeo was very young, before the peach, before the System, before anything, Old Man Shen had a toothache that lasted the whole of a winter.

He chewed bitterroot for it. He sat by the fire in the hut on the hill with the green pulp packed in his cheek and his face pulled sideways like a fish's, and the dog lay on his feet to keep them warm, and the old man talked to him, because there was no one else to talk to.

"You want to know where you got your name?" he said one night, though the dog had not asked, being at the time a dog. "Story I heard when I was a boy. Fellow came through selling needles, from somewhere so far west the sun goes there to sleep. Told it all down the valley. Young fool falls in love, loves so hard and so fast he doesn't stop to look where he's running, and it kills him." He spat green into the fire. "That was his name. Romeo."

The dog thumped his tail, because his name had been said.

"There. That's why." The old man's hand came down on his head, light and dry. "You run after everything. You love every fool who drops you a crust. You'll get your heart broken a hundred times, you long fool, and you'll go right on doing it." A pause. The fire popped. "Good. Better that than the other way."

Three thousand years later, Romeo still did not know if the old man had been right about the hundred times.

It had been a great many more than that.

---

Ling Mei lay on the floor of the hut with her cheek on the boards and her right arm down the entrance shaft up to the shoulder, and grumbled, and then stopped grumbling.

Romeo lay at the bottom of the shaft with his hind end in the new trough and his long belly pressed flat along the vein, and his nose against the inside of her wrist.

The earth qi came up into him slowly. It always came slowly. It was the slowest thing he knew: cool and heavy, patient as clay, with no hurry in it anywhere. It did not want to go anywhere. It only wanted to lie where it lay. He let it come to his nose, the way the old man had taught him to let everything come, and then, instead of keeping it, he let it go on through him: along his belly, up his chest, through the long column of his neck, out through the tip of his nose and into the hot, crowded river of her arm.

She flinched. Then she went still.

*Out first,* he said. *All the way.*

She breathed out.

The tide in her met the earth qi the way a flood meets a riverbed. It did not stop. Nothing could stop it. But it found, for the first time, something beneath it that would not move, something cool and heavy and patient enough to push against without breaking, and it began, very slowly, to push against that instead of against her.

Romeo turned it.

He had been turning her meridians by main force for three nights, one cupful at a time. Tonight he was not doing it alone. Tonight the mountain was doing most of the work, and he was only showing it where to lean. It was the difference between pushing a cart and holding the reins.

It still hurt her. Twice her fingers spasmed against his muzzle, and once she made a small sound into the floorboards that he pretended not to hear. But she was not sick into a bowl, and she did not curl up, and at some point in the hour of the rat her breath went long and even and she said, wonderingly, into the dark, "It's cold. Romeo, it's *cold*, in my arm. It's never been cold."

*That is the mountain,* said Romeo. *Do not thank it. It does not care.*

His own legs were shaking. He had not been ready for that. Qi that passes through a body leaves a little of itself behind, and also takes a little of the body with it, and his channels were a puppy's channels: narrow, new, and not built to be a road for anything heavier than his own small sea. He felt them ache along the whole length of his spine, down the long back that was his weakness in every body he had ever had.

He did not stop. He had been praising her courage in silence for three nights. It seemed only fair to return the favour.

"Six," Ling Mei whispered. She was half asleep already, her face soft and slack against the boards. "Six, Romeo."

*Six,* said Romeo, and lay on the thinned, exhausted thread of earth qi, and tried to stop his legs from shaking.

---

He did not sleep. He lay in the trough with his chin on his paws and thought about Brow.

A bound beast could not lie to its master. A binding did not forbid a beast to think, or to keep its own counsel. It only forbade it to refuse. Ask a bound wolf a question and the answer came up out of it like water from a pump, whether the wolf wished it or not. But no one could ask a question they had not thought of.

*He has not asked me yet.*

That had not been a threat. Romeo had smelled a great many threats. That had been a wolf telling him exactly how long he had, as precisely as it was able, and then lying back down on its master's terrace in a bronze collar and waiting to see what a very small dog would do with it.

Which raised the question of why.

He was turning that over when he noticed the smell in the vein.

It was faint. He had been lying on this thread of earth qi for three nights and had never caught it, because there had never been this little of the vein left: a scent buried under the clay smell, the way the bottom of a well is buried under the water. Now the vein was thin, scraped down nearly to nothing by the night's work, and under it, carried down from somewhere far up the hill by the rainwater seam, was something else.

Lake mud. Cold, deep, unmoving water. Moss on stone that has not been turned over in a thousand years.

And old shell.

Romeo's head came up off his paws so fast his ears flapped against the walls of the trough.

He knew that smell. He had known it for three thousand years. He had last smelled it nine hundred years ago, on a sandbar in the second tier, over a game of go that had lasted eleven years and ended with a very old turtle saying, slowly, *I am going to sleep now, dog. Do not wake me for anything less than the end of the world,* and then sinking into a lake without another word.

The rainwater seam came down the hill. The vein came down the seam. And somewhere at the top of it, in the second tier, under a lake, something very old was still breathing.

His tail was wagging. He did not tell it to.

---

A little before dawn, he went out the back-door tunnel to the reeds, because some needs are older than cultivation, and found that the reeds on the ditch bank had already been flattened by something enormous.

Brow was lying in them.

The great blue-white wolf lay with his chin on his forepaws and the bronze collar dull with frost, and his pale eyes were already open and already on the tunnel mouth. Romeo had not smelled him. Romeo, who could smell a lie across a courtyard and a pill through a leather pouch, had come out of the ground with the wind at his back and had not smelled a wolf the size of a cart lying in the reeds ten paces away.

*You chose your wind,* Romeo said.

*I have the best nose in the kennels, small dog,* said Brow, in his rusty-gate voice. *It means I know where everyone else's is.* The pale eyes did not blink. *He asked me a question last night.*

Romeo's hackles went up all along his back, the whole length of him, the way they had for the stone.

*Not the right one,* said Brow. *He asked whether the girl's dog smelled of anything but dog. I told him the truth. You smelled of bitterroot, bathwater, and dog.* A long breath, steaming in the cold. *That is not why I came.*

*Then why?*

*A sealed slip came down the mountain at midnight. He read it in front of me.* Brow lifted his great head from his paws. *The collector is not coming in seven days. The collector is coming in four.*

# **Chapter 23: The Bucket Book**

Ling Mei did not put him down, and she did not run. Romeo would remember that about her later. Her arms had gone hard as a trap around him, and her heart was going under his ribs like a hare's, but her feet stayed exactly where they were in the snow.  
The voice in the slot waited, the way a man waits who is used to being answered.  
When no answer came, it went on, mild and unhurried, as if remarking on the weather from a warm room.  
"The river was high that year too," it said. "You went in after the hat. He told you not to, and you went in anyway."  
Romeo's whole body locked.  
There had been a hat. Straw, wide, mended at the brim with red thread. A gust had taken it off Shen's head on the bank and put it in the brown water, and a small dog with no name and no sense had gone in after it and been rolled over and over and come out half a li downstream with the hat in his jaws and the river in his lungs. Shen had sat on the bank laughing and coughing at the same time and said, *Well. What am I going to call you, then?*  
Nobody alive knew about the hat. Romeo had never told it. Not to Bao, not to anyone, in three thousand years.  
"I'll keep it for you," said the voice, pleasantly. "The river. Until you want it."  
Then the slot was only a slot again, a dark mouth under a boulder, and the smell of silk and lamp smoke drew back down into the mountain like a tide going out.

"Romeo," Ling Mei breathed. "What hat?"  
*Walk,* he said. *Please. Just walk.*  
---

She walked.  
The way back over the white shoulder of the mountain was the way they had come in the dark, but daylight made it a different country. In the dark there had been only the next step and his nose. Now there was distance, a great deal of it, bright and flat and open, and nowhere on it for a girl in a dark coat to hide.  
Romeo rode inside the coat with his head out under her chin and did the only work he could still do. He read the wind.  
*Left,* he would say, and she would go left, along the lee of a drift where the snow was firm. *Not that way. Something has been digging there and it has bigger teeth than me.* Once, near noon, he smelled woodsmoke and wet felt coming up the far side of the ridge, and he made her lie flat behind a hummock of rock while two woodcutters argued their way past about whose turn it was to carry the axe. They never looked up. People looking for trouble on a mountain look for something taller than a hummock.  
The trouble was not the woodcutters. The trouble was her.  
Bao's water qi had steadied her tide and warmed her through, but warmth is not strength, and she had carried him over this mountain once already without sleep. By early afternoon she was stopping every hundred steps. By mid-afternoon it was every fifty, and she had stopped talking, and her left arm, the one that would not straighten, had begun to shake under him.  
At the next stop Romeo wriggled out of her coat before she could catch him and dropped into the snow.  
It came up to his shoulders. He had expected that. He had not expected a crust on top that his chest broke through with every step, so that walking was less like walking than like swimming through a field of broken plates.  
"Get back in the coat," said Ling Mei.  
*I am walking,* said Romeo, and ploughed forward a full body-length, and stopped, and sneezed. *I am the correct length for this. The snow is wrong.*  
"You're up to your ears."  
*My ears are long. It's a design feature.*  
He ploughed on. Behind him he heard her get up, and he heard that she walked easier with nothing in her arms, and he lowered his head and ploughed, a small red plough with a white tip on its tail, breaking a trench for a girl who did not need one. He did it for nearly a li before his back began to talk to him in the voice it had used nine hundred years ago, the year it broke, and he stopped and stood very still in the trench with his legs braced, waiting for it to decide.  
Ling Mei picked him up without a word and put him back inside her coat.  
*I was resting,* he said.  
"I know."  
*Precious cargo,* he added, after a while, into the warm wool.  
"I know," she said again, and he felt her chin come down on top of his head and stay there.  
---

He told her about the hat on the long slope down through the black pines, because she had asked, and because he could not stop thinking about it.  
He told it plainly. The bank, the gust, the brown water, the half li of being rolled like a stone. Shen laughing and coughing. *What am I going to call you, then.* He did not tell her it was the day he got his name, but she worked that out for herself, and her arms tightened.  
"And you never told anyone."  
*Not one soul.*  
"Then how..." She stopped. He felt her think it through, the way she thought through everything, slowly and all the way to the end. "It's not the inspector, is it. It's something that sounds like him. And it knows about the hat because it has the river. Your river smell. The bit the stone took." She swallowed. "It's wearing your memory like a coat."  
Romeo said nothing for twenty steps.  
*Bao thinks it has no name of its own,* he said at last. *A pot with no hole in the middle. So it borrows. The inspector's voice. Shen's way of saying my name. My river.* He put his nose deeper into her collar. *And every borrowed thing it holds, it holds the way a man holds a bone over a dog's head.*  
"To make you jump."  
*To make me go in,* said Romeo. *I always went in. It knows that too, now.*  
---

They came down out of the pines a little before dusk, between the last two storehouses, the way they had left, and the first thing Romeo smelled was the junior steward.  
Boiled turnip, ink, a thin anxious sweat, and the cold-iron tang of the well rope. The junior steward stood at the door of the water house with a narrow bound book open on his forearm and a brush in his hand, and he was not counting buckets. He was waiting.  
"Ling Mei," he said, and wrote something, and looked up. He was a thin young man, hardly older than she was, who had been given exactly one small power in the world and kept it very clean. "Hour of the snake. Twelve buckets. I have zero buckets."  
"I know, Steward. I'm sorry."  
"Where were you?"  
Romeo felt her draw breath. He had not told her what to say. He had trusted her to think of something, and he had a moment, inside her coat, to wonder whether he had been a fool.  
"My dog ran off," said Ling Mei.  
The steward's brush stopped.  
"Up past the storehouses, early. After something. I went after him and the snow came down and I got turned around in the pines and I couldn't find the way back till it got light." All true, Romeo noted, in its way. She had simply left out a mountain. "I'll make up the buckets."  
The steward looked at the coat. Romeo put his head out of it.  
The steward looked at him for a long moment: the long red body, the snow balled in the feathering of his ears, the crusted split above one eye, the short legs dangling.  
"That," he said, "ran up a mountain."  
There was a single stone step up into the water house, not much higher than a man's ankle, and Romeo did the thing a dachshund does when it has been carried for half a day and is put down in front of a step.  
He wriggled out of the coat, landed, shook himself so hard that snow spattered the open page of the bucket book, marched with great dignity to the step, put his front paws on it, and could not get his back end up after them.  
He tried twice. On the second try he hung there, front half on the step and back half on the ground, hind legs bicycling against the stone, long belly scraping, tail going round like a little mill wheel, and he gave one deep, outraged bark that was much too big for him and echoed off the storehouses.  
It was not entirely acting. His back really would not do it.  
The junior steward made a sound through his nose. Then another one. Then he put the brush behind his ear and laughed, helplessly, with his hand over his mouth, the way people laugh who have not had cause to for some time.  
"Ran up a mountain," he said, "and couldn't get down again. I believe it." He wiped his eyes and blew the snow off his page and wrote, and Romeo could smell the ink: a short line, and no more. "Twenty-four buckets tomorrow. Twelve and twelve. And keep that sausage on a string."  
He fished in his sleeve and held out a length of plain hemp cord.  
Romeo saw it coming toward his neck in Ling Mei's hand, and every hair on his body stood up at once, and he felt the flinch rise in him like sickness, the old silver flinch from three thousand years ago, *Sit*, and the lightning.  
The cord did not go round his neck.  
Ling Mei knelt in the snow, lifted his front end off the step, passed the cord behind his front legs and round his chest where his ribs were deepest, and tied it there with the same slip-knot she used on the blue lunch cloth, loose enough to put two fingers under. Then she wrapped the other end twice around her own wrist.  
"He doesn't like things round his neck," she said to the steward.  
"It's a dog," said the steward, but he said it kindly, and closed his book.  
Romeo stood in his loose hemp harness and looked up at her, and did not say anything into her mind at all, because there was nothing in three thousand years of words that would have been the right size.  
---

The assessment stone stood in the middle of the outer yard where it always stood, waist-high and tooth-shaped in the blue dusk, its silver band catching the last of the light. They had to pass it to reach the huts.  
The still escort was on duty.  
He stood beside the stone with his spear grounded and his lacquer mask turned toward the gate, and Romeo, trotting at the end of his cord with his nose low, read him from forty paces off.  
Lacquer. Leather. Horse. Silver, much silver. Fear, as always, old fear, worn into him like a groove into a step. All of that was the same. The river smell was gone from his palm; Romeo had expected that.  
What he had not expected was the escort's boots. They were wet to the ankle, which meant nothing in this snow, but the wet was not snow-wet. It was cave-wet, the clinging mineral damp of a place no sun had ever reached, and caked into the welt of the left boot was a fine grey dust that smelled, faintly, unmistakably, of silk and lamp smoke.  
The dust of the dry passage. Fresh. From today.  
Romeo did not change his pace. He trotted past the stone like any small foolish dog coming home late, and as he came level, the mask turned. Not toward Ling Mei. Down, toward him.  
Under the lacquer, Romeo smelled the man's fear jump, and then, astonishingly, settle into something else. Something he had smelled on soldiers before, in the evening after a battle, when they counted heads and found one more than they had dared hope for.  
Relief.  
The mask turned back to the gate. Neither Romeo nor Ling Mei breathed properly until the huts were between them and the stone.  
*He went up the passage today,* Romeo said. *Into the dark, where that thing was. And he came down and stood his watch.* He was quiet a moment. *And he is glad I didn't come.*  
---

Ling Mei's hut was dark and very cold, and smelled of yesterday's charcoal and her blanket, and Romeo's whole body had begun to ache toward the earth-qi trough under the floor the way a thirsty dog aches toward water.  
He crossed to the third floorboard and stopped.  
It did not sit a little proud. It sat flat. Perfectly, carefully flat, the way a board sits when someone who does not know its habits has lifted it and put it back.  
Romeo put his nose to the crack. Earth. His own scent. Old wool. And over them, fresh and unmistakable in a space that small: stoat. And bitter herbs, and old paper, and a very old man with one eye squinted shut.  
Something moved in the dark below, and a narrow, whiskered face came up between the boards, and two bright black eyes regarded him.  
*About time,* said Smoke. *He's been down there all day. Your back tunnel is very nice, by the way. The new one's nicer.* The stoat's nose twitched. *He says to tell you he knows what the stone is. He says he's known for a month. And he says if you're going back to the lake before the moon goes, you'd better hear the rest first.*

# **Chapter 29: Not One Word Out Loud**

The junior steward would not go away. He stood in the lane outside the hut, shifting from foot to foot in the snow, and every few breaths he said "Ling Mei?" through the door in the voice of a man who has been told to come back with someone and does not want to find out what happens if he comes back without her.  
"I'm washing my face," Ling Mei told him.  
"You don't have any water."  
"I'm washing it anyway."  
She knelt on the third floorboard, lifted it, and looked down the shaft into the dark, where two amber-hazel eyes looked back up at her from the bottom.  
"You said in the morning," she whispered. "It's afternoon. Tell me now. The short way."  
So he told her the short way, in the time it takes a girl to retie her sash with shaking fingers. The man in the drain. The lie, and where it had sat. The cold hand that had reached for his neck all by itself, and the promise behind it: *bring me the little dog, up the passage, on the dark night*. And what Romeo had scratched in the silt in answer.  
Ling Mei's hands stopped on her sash.  
"You said yes," she whispered. "To a trap."  
*To a map,* said Romeo. *A trap you know about is a map with teeth. It tells me where the thing will be standing on the one night I most want to know.*  
"That isn't how maps work."  
*It is how mine work.*  
He could smell her deciding not to argue with him in front of a steward, and filing the argument away somewhere safe, for later, the way he buried things.  
"Fine," she said. "Can you climb?"  
He could not climb. They both knew it. His back had spent the morning making private remarks, and some of them had been in language he had not used since the Thousand-Li Ice Road.  
So he went out the back way instead, forty level paces of tunnel on his belly to the ditch reeds, and when he put his head out into the snow she was already crouched there waiting, having told the steward she had dropped something. She got her good right hand under his chest and her crooked left arm under his long red middle, and lifted him out of the reeds like a bucket out of a well.  
*I am not luggage,* said Romeo, dangling.  
"You are today," said Ling Mei, and tucked him into the front of her padded coat.  
---
It was a long way up the hill to the Beast Hall, and it was the first time Romeo had gone there in daylight without bitterroot on his tongue.  
Twice before he had been under Fang Wuji's spiritual sense. Once he had been numb with bitterroot; once so cold and spent that his little ember could pass for a sick dog's warmth. Now he was Level 3, fed on earth qi all night, and his sea was small but it was not empty. Anyone who looked properly would see a dog who had been cultivating.  
And Fang Wuji always looked properly.  
They passed the stone. It stood in the middle of the yard, its silver band dull under a white sky. Two masked men stood beside it. One was broad and stamped his feet. The other was very still, and his right hand was tucked deep into his belt, and as Ling Mei passed he did not turn his head at all. But Romeo smelled him: lacquer, leather, horse, old sweat, and fear, a day old and holding.  
*Good,* thought Romeo. *Keep it there.*  
The steward led them through the cedar hall, and Romeo braced for the short passage to the lamplit chamber and the altar. But they did not go that way. They went up. Up a narrow stair, every step a small jolt in his spine, to a door at the top, where the steward knocked once, bowed at the wood, said "Outer disciple Ling Mei, Elder, and, ah, the sausage," and fled back down.  
---
The room was plain. A writing table. A brazier. A window of real glass, very old and a little wavy, that looked straight down over the outer yard and the stone. Brow lay along the far wall, taking up most of it, chin on his paws, pale eyes open.  
Fang Wuji stood at the window with his back to them.  
He did not turn round. His spiritual sense came off him the way cold comes off a lake, and it filled the room, and it found the girl in the doorway, and then it found the dog inside her coat.  
Romeo had perhaps the time of one breath.  
He had no bitterroot, and no ground under him to pour himself into. What he had was a bond, a thing that was not a binding, that ran from his chest to hers, and down which, night after night, he had drawn earth qi up into her in thin, careful threads.  
He reversed it.  
He took the whole of his small, warm sea and pushed it down the bond into Ling Mei, all at once.  
She made a sound like a stepped-on cat and caught at the doorframe.  
The qi went into her and did what everything did in Ling Mei: it went the wrong way. It sank into the backward tide of her meridians and was carried down and in, round and under, a little warm current lost in a great slow cold one. To a sense sweeping from outside, it would look like the girl's own tide, turning over in its sleep.  
In her coat, Romeo was a dog with nothing in him. A dog with less qi than a chicken.

*Oh,* thought Romeo. *Oh, no.*  
Fang Wuji's sense touched the dog, found nothing, and moved on.  
It did not leave Ling Mei.  
"Disciple." The elder turned from the window at last. Pine resin, old ink, and that tiredness, deeper than at the hut, gone into the grain of him like rain into wood. "Close the door. Sit."  
Romeo's haunches tried to drop inside her coat. He sat on them.  
Ling Mei closed the door. She sat on the mat in front of the table, carefully, the way you sit when something hot is moving round inside your chest, and lifted Romeo out and set him beside her knee. He lay down at once, chin on his paws, a dog of no account, and did not look at the wolf.  
Fang Wuji sat too. He looked at her for some time.  
"When you brought this animal to be registered," he said, "your tide did not move at all. I took the trouble to look. It was as still as a pond in a jar." His eyes did not leave her face. "Today it is moving."  
"I've been drawing buckets, Elder," said Ling Mei. "Twenty-four a day."  
It was perfectly true, and it was so obviously not the reason that Romeo nearly sneezed.  
Fang Wuji's mouth did something small at one corner. It might have been the beginning of a smile, if he had been a man who finished them.  
"Keep drawing them," he said. "That is not why I sent for you."  
---
He laid one hand on the ruff of the wolf's neck. Brow had come silently down the length of the wall to lie at the elder's side, and the binding between them, which Romeo could smell in any room as a long cold note, drew suddenly taut.  
"Two nights ago," said Fang Wuji, "something stood under that window."  
The room went very quiet.  
"It left footprints. Straw sandals. Toes out. A drag behind every right heel." He said it the way a man reads out a ledger. "They came from nowhere in the middle of the yard and went to nowhere at the foot of my wall. I have seen the same prints before. They stop at the back of your hut, disciple. I went and looked."  
Ling Mei said nothing. Romeo, very slowly, let one ear fall over his eye, which is what a bored dog does, and under it he listened with every hair.  
"And it spoke," said Fang Wuji. "It had a voice like an old man reading out a will. Dry. Very sure of itself. Rather pleased with its own vocabulary."  
In her lap, Ling Mei's right hand began to tremble. She put her left over it.  
Romeo knew that voice. He had heard it two nights ago, too, coming up a shaft, asking a girl to lift a board.  
It was his.  
"So I will ask you a plain question, and you will answer it plainly, because Brow will tell me if you don't." The hand on the ruff did not move. "Has that animal ever spoken to you?"  
Brow's pale eyes opened fully and fixed on the girl.  
Romeo could not say a word to her. Not here. Not with the leash pulled tight and Fang's hand on it. He lay with his chin on his paws and did the hardest thing a dog can do, which is nothing, while someone he loves walks out onto ice.  
Ling Mei looked down at the dog, and thought about it for exactly as long as an honest person would.  
"Elder," she said, "he has never said one word out loud to me in his whole life. He barks. He barks at the steward and at nothing at night. He's very rude."  
Every word of it was true.  
Romeo smelled it go up the leash. It went up the cold line from wolf to master the way a bead goes up a string, plain and unbent, and it said: *true*.

Fang Wuji closed his eyes. When he opened them something had gone out of him, a held thing, and Romeo could not tell whether it was relief or disappointment, because it smelled of both.  
"No," he said quietly. "I didn't think so. Those are not words a dog would choose."  
---
"What did it say?" asked Ling Mei.  
The elder looked at her as if he had forgotten she could talk.  
Then, to Romeo's very great surprise, he answered.  
"It said, *You were promised the wolf would be last, Wuji. You were lied to.*"  
His hand had tightened in Brow's ruff. The wolf did not seem to mind.  
"I knew what that stone was before it came off the cart," said Fang Wuji. He said it to the window, not to her. "Every sect on the first tier has one now, disciple, or will have before the spring. The ones that refused are not sects any longer. I was told that if the Azure Wolf Sect was quiet, and orderly, and kept a clean ledger, then the stone would take what it takes and the last name on its list would be this one." He did not look down at the wolf. He did not have to. "I have kept him two hundred and six years. Things that are mine, I keep. I was prepared to pay a great deal for that." A pause. "I have paid it."  
"On the night the moon is dark," said Fang Wuji, "the inspector wishes every beast in this sect chained at a post in the yard, around the stone, from dusk until dawn. Every beast on the ledger." His eyes came down to the small red dog lying by the girl's knee. "Yours is on the ledger."  
"Yes, Elder."  
"He will not be in the yard." The elder said it as if he were reading the weather. "He will be ill. The physician will say so. You will bring him here, to this room, at dusk, and you will both stay in it, with Brow and with me, until it is light. If something in straw sandals wants anything that is in my ledger, it will have to come up those stairs and ask me for it."  
Romeo, who on the dark night needed very much to be at the top of a passage on the far side of the mountain, put his chin a little harder on his paws.  
"Elder," said Ling Mei carefully. "Why him?"  
"Because its prints stop at your wall," said Fang Wuji, "and I would like to know why. And because the inspector, who has not taken his gloves off once since he rode in, not to eat, not to write, not to warm his hands at my brazier, asked me this morning, very pleasantly, how your dog was sleeping." He stood. The interview was over. "Go and draw your buckets, disciple."  
---
She carried him down the stair with her heart going like a rabbit's, and neither of them said anything until they were out in the white afternoon and halfway across the yard, with the stone behind them.  
Then, so low that only a dog's long ears would have caught it: "I didn't lie."  
*No,* said Romeo. *You were magnificent. You were also very nearly sick on an elder's mat. I'm sorry. That was my qi. I'll take it back tonight.*  
"It's warm," she whispered. "It's still going round. It feels like swallowing soup the wrong way." A pause. "He saw it."  
*He saw your tide move.* Romeo laid his chin on her collarbone. *He thinks it was buckets.*  
"He does not think it was buckets."  
*No,* Romeo admitted. *He doesn't.*

He read it twice. Then he did not tell her, because she would have asked how, and the answer was *by being frightened and full of a dog*, and he did not think she would find that encouraging.  
Behind them, faintly, as they reached the huts, a voice came after him down the hill. Not up a leash. Not to Fang. Mind to mind, only to the small dog, like snow sliding off a roof.  
*Small dog.*  
Romeo did not turn his head. *Brow.*  
*He did not tell her all of it.* The wolf's voice was very even, the way a voice is even when it is carrying something heavy. *The thing at the window said one more word. He thinks it was nonsense. An old word for snow, in a tongue no one in this sect has spoken for five hundred years.*  
Romeo's tail went still against Ling Mei's ribs.  
*It was not nonsense,* said Brow. *It was my name. The one under the coat. The one I have never said aloud to anyone.*  
A pause, long and cold.  
*And it said it in my mother's voice.*

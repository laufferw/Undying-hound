# **Chapter 15: The Name He Comes To**

The candle went out a little after midnight. Ling Mei did not light another. She sat in the dark with her back against the wall and the blanket round both of them, and after a while she said, "Now."

It was not a question. Romeo had known she would not let him reach morning without it, and he had spent the last hour of shivering deciding where to begin. There were three thousand years to choose from. In the end he began where all the trouble had.

*I ate a peach,* he said into her mind.

There was a pause. "A peach."

*A spirit peach. It fell out of a tree that should not have been growing on the bank where it grew, and I was a puppy, and it was there. That was three thousand one hundred and twelve years ago.* He felt her go still under him. *I'm not a stray, Ling Mei. I'm very old. I have been very strong. I was at the Ninth Gate once, the last step before immortality, and something above the clouds put a chain round my neck and told me to sit, and the lightning took everything. I woke up in a ditch at the bottom of your sect with the legs I had when I was young.*

He told her the rest in order, because order was the only kindness he had to offer. The cold slope and the saddle. The lake, and the old turtle under it, Bao, ten thousand years old and sarcastic about every one of them, sewn to the lakebed by twenty-seven silver threads. The three threads that had parted when Romeo bit them. The long dark ride down the seam inside the mountain, and the pin wedged in the rock below the black-pine spring, and the tall narrow thing with the lamp that walked the dry passage beside the water, tapping its pins and listening, and had not smelled him because he had been too cold to be anything at all.

He told her about the shaving at his throat. *Every bound beast in this sect smells of silver. I don't. So I borrowed some. Bao's advice: wear a little of what you hate.*

Ling Mei did not interrupt once. When he finished, she was quiet for so long that he began to smell her deciding something, and he braced for it the way he had braced for Fang Wuji's fingers. Fear, he expected. Or the particular hurt of someone who has been lied to by a dog.

"Three thousand years," she said at last.

*And a hundred and twelve.*

"And you still can't get onto the bed by yourself."

*The sleeping platform,* said Romeo with dignity, *is a special case.*

She laughed. It came out of her cracked and too loud in the dark, and she put her hand over her mouth, and then she was crying a little as well, without any fuss, the way she did everything. He smelled no fear in it. He smelled relief, and anger on his behalf, and under both a stubborn warm thing that he had smelled on very few people in three thousand years and had never once managed to stop himself loving.

"You should have told me before you went up the mountain," she said.

*Yes.*

"You could have died up there."

He considered several answers. He chose the true one, or most of it. *I've been killed before, Ling Mei. It has never quite taken.* He felt the question rising in her and put his chin on her wrist before she could ask it. *Not tonight. That's a story for when we have a fire and a sausage.*

---

They planned the way poor people plan, with what was in the room.

"The cloth stays on," said Ling Mei. "The elder told me to keep it on you. If you turn up without it in the morning, he'll want to know why, and so will his wolf."

*The System says what marks me as held may be followed by those who hold.*

"Then it's a bad idea."

*It's a bad idea that's already been seen by a Nascent Soul elder. Taking it off would be a worse one.*

She chewed her lip. "Then we keep it, and we know. That's different from keeping it and not knowing." She shifted him on her lap. "Now the stone."

That was the real problem, and they both knew it. The assessment stone did not read a beast's qi or its realm. It read the name a beast most deeply turned toward, and it took that name, and kept it, and glowed with it in front of everyone. A fox had stood before it and given up something pretty and true, and two days later she had been sold.

Romeo had a name beneath his name. It was older than Shen, older than the peach, the sound a thing makes for itself before anyone else has made a sound for it. He had never said it aloud. He did not know whether he still turned toward it, or whether, in front of a stone and a collector and a hundred watching disciples, it would rise up out of him on its own.

"Romeo," said Ling Mei.

His tail thumped against her knee.

She was silent a moment. Then, slowly: "Romeo."

Thump.

"You didn't decide to do that."

*My tail has never consulted me about anything.*

"That's what I mean." He could hear her working it out, the same way she had worked out how to carry water with one good hand. "You said the stone reads what you turn toward. Not what you think. Your tail gets there before you do. Every time anyone says it." She put her bad hand on his back, along the red saddle of it. "So don't think in front of the stone. Just be a dog. Let me call you. Let your tail answer."

It was so simple that for a moment he did not trust it. Then, because he was too tired to hold anything up any longer, he slept.

---

He dreamed of a river in a valley that no longer had a name, and of an old man sitting on the bank with his trouser legs rolled up.

The puppy was on the far side of the shallows, long and absurd, with too much ear and not enough leg, his nose full of the thousand interesting smells of the reeds. He had been called many things that summer. Dog. Thief. Get-out-of-that. The man was trying something new.

"Romeo," said Old Man Shen.

The puppy did not look up. There was a frog.

"Romeo," said Shen, as patiently as the river. He said it again, and again, all afternoon, not loudly, the way a man drops pebbles into water. He did not chase. He did not grab. When the light went gold, the puppy finally came splashing across the shallows, not because he had decided to, but because his legs had, and his tail had, and somewhere in the course of the afternoon the sound had stopped being a noise the old man made and become a place to go.

Shen put a hand on his wet head. "There," he said. "That's all a name is. It's not a thing you're given. It's the thing you come to."

---

Romeo woke with his nose full of wolf.

The grey before dawn was in the cracks of the shutters. Ling Mei had fallen asleep sitting up, her head against the wall, one hand still on his back. He was warm. He was, for the first time since the lake, not shivering.

And outside the door, lying in the snow, was Brow.

Romeo slid out from under Ling Mei's hand with the slow care of a dog leaving a bed he is not supposed to be on. The drop from the platform jarred his long sore back all the way down. He nosed the door; it opened a crack on its loose latch, and cold came in, and he went out into it.

The snow had drifted in the night. It came up to his chest at the first step and to his chin at the second, and he did what he had always done with snow, which was to put his head down and go through it like a small red plough, with tremendous resolve and almost no grace, until he arrived, panting and powdered white from ears to tail, beside an enormous wolf who had been watching the entire performance without blinking.

They looked at each other. Then Romeo did what he had not dared to do in the hut.

*Why?* he said into the wolf's mind.

Brow did not startle. Perhaps he had expected it. When his answer came, it came slowly, from far down, in the voice of a creature that had not spoken in its own words for a very long time. It was cold and wide and smelled, very faintly, of a name with snow in it.

*She told me her name through the kennel wall,* said Brow. *The fox. The night before the stone. Bright-Tail-Under-the-Bamboo. She said someone should have it who would not sell it. Then the stone had it. Then she was gone.* The pale eyes did not move from Romeo's face. *I could not keep her. I thought I would see if I could keep you.*

Romeo found he had nothing clever to say. He let his nose say it instead: he leaned in, slowly, and touched it to the side of the wolf's great jaw, the way one dog touches another, and held it there a moment.

*Your master,* he said. *Did he believe you?*

The wolf's ears went back. *He has not believed me since the fox,* said Brow. *He knew I lied tonight. He felt it on the leash.* A long breath of steam. *He let it stand.*

Romeo turned that over and did not like any of its sides. Before he could ask the next question, Brow lifted his head and turned it toward the east, toward the notch in the white ridge where the pass came down, and every hair along the wolf's back rose at once.

Romeo smelled it a breath later.

Horses, three. Leather. Oil. The cold iron smell of cage-bars carried a long way. And over all of it, bright and thin and enormous, so much of it that it made his teeth sing in his jaw, silver: not a pin's worth, not a shaving's worth, but chains of it, coils of it, enough to bind a hundred beasts.

At his throat, folded in Ling Mei's blue lunch cloth, the little curl of silver grew warm. It stirred against his neck, the way a needle stirs on a thread, and turned, very gently, toward the pass.

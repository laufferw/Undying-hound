# **Chapter 11: Knock Louder**

Romeo did not move.  
It was the oldest rule of going to ground, older than sects, older than the System, older than the country of short-legged dogs his body remembered and he did not. When something is at the mouth of your burrow, you do not turn your back on it. He lay at the dead end of his tunnel with his chin on Bao's shell and the lake pressing on the clay above him, and he watched the yellow light lying across the gravel thirty dog-lengths behind him, and he waited for it to come in.  
It did not come in.  
The tunnel was the width of one small dog. The thing on the shore was tall and narrow, but it was not that narrow, and it did not seem inclined to kneel in wet gravel. The light grew no closer. It only settled, the way lantern light settles when the lantern has been set down on the ground.  
Then the voice came down the tunnel. It was light and unhurried and belonged to no country Romeo knew, and it did not echo, though everything echoed down here.  
"Come out, little one," it said. "You'll catch your death down there."  
Romeo breathed in through his nose, slowly, the full length of the tunnel. Silk. Lamp smoke. Silver. Wet gravel, his own blood, and old shell. Nothing else. No fear, no anger, no hunger, no pity. He had smelled corpses with more intent in them.  
There was a pause on the shore, of the kind a person makes when they have remembered something.  
"No," said the voice, thoughtfully. "You won't, will you?"  
The hair rose along Romeo's back from his neck to the white tip of his tail, and stayed there.  
Nobody knew. Nobody living. Shen had known, and Shen was three thousand years in his grave, and the grave was the one place in the Ninefold Ascent Romeo had never told anyone about. If the Hound-Catchers had ever known, they would have hunted him very differently. Bao knew, and Bao was under him, numb to the feet.  
*It's guessing,* Romeo told himself. *It's a thing that ties up turtles in the dark. It says strange things to frighten small animals out of holes.*  
His nose, which had never once in three thousand years agreed to lie for him, said nothing at all.  
On the shore, the voice sighed, quite kindly.  
"Well," it said. "Stay, then. It makes no difference."  
And the thread above Romeo's head was plucked again.  
---

Not once, this time. Slowly, over and over, the way you might rock a cradle with one finger. Each note slid down through the black water and past him into the depths, and at the bottom of it Romeo felt Bao's great slow mind dip, and rise a little less, and dip again.  
*Old shell.*  
Nothing. Then, very far away: *...mm.*  
*Bao. Stay awake.*  
*Is it...* The thought trailed off. *I was dreaming of silk.*  
*It's not a dream. It's a lullaby, and it's for you, and you're not a hatchling. Wake up.*  
Pluck. Pluck. The stone under his chin was cooling again.  
Romeo lowered his head to butt the shell, and stopped. The skin above his eye was already split. If he knocked hard enough to matter, he would knock himself senseless, and then there would be two of them asleep at the bottom of the lake, and only one of them was ten thousand years old and could afford it.  
*Knock louder,* Bao had said, on a mud bank, two thousand years ago. *If it's the end of the world.*  
Romeo had no hammer and no hands. But there was one thing a badger-dog had that was louder than the rest of him put together, and the men who had bred his far-off ancestors had wanted it that way on purpose: so that when the little dog was deep in the earth, face to face with the badger, the hunters above could hear him through the ground and know where to dig.  
He pressed his chest flat against the shell, so that his ribs and the stone were one thing. He opened his jaws.  
And he barked.  
It was not a puppy's bark. It came up out of three thousand years of being underestimated. It hit the end of the tunnel and had nowhere to go but into the stone, so it went into the stone, and through it, and down. Romeo barked again, and again, three times, then two, a heron walking, and then he stopped counting and simply barked, the way his kind bark at the bottom of a burrow when they mean to be found or die there, until his throat was raw and his ears rang and the blood from his eye ran into his mouth.  
On the shore, the plucking stopped.  
And under him, the end of the world answered.  
---

It started as a sound below hearing. Romeo felt it first in his teeth and then in his long back, a vast slow grinding, like a mountain thinking about turning over in its sleep. The clay above him cracked from end to end with a noise like a whip. Gravel poured from the walls.  
Then the shell lifted.  
Not far. A hand's breadth, perhaps, at the rim. But the rim of Old Turtle Bao was the size of a sect's outer wall, and when it rose a hand's breadth, the lake bed rose with it. Somewhere overhead the ice broke along the whole length of the shore with a boom that Romeo felt in his bowels. Through the tunnel came a sound of water heaving up over gravel and snow, and a hiss, and the yellow light at the mouth of the tunnel shuddered, guttered, and went out.  
The thread above his head jerked. Then it went slack and did not tighten again. Further off, in the dark water, he heard two more go, faint as hair snapping, torn out of their pins on some far valley seam by the weight of a turtle shrugging.  
Then the lake came into the tunnel.  
It came through the cracked clay all at once, black and cold beyond cold, and filled the space around him to his chest before he could turn. Romeo did what every dachshund does in deep water, which was to swim with tremendous energy and very little result. His short legs churned. His long body sank in the middle like a wet rope. The lunch bundle dragged at his neck. Above him the tunnel's roof was a hand's breadth away, then a finger's, then nothing.  
*Under,* said Bao. The voice was no longer far away. It was everywhere, slow and enormous and annoyed. *Not up. Under. Come under, you ridiculous animal.*  
Under was the one direction Romeo had never in his life refused. He shut his eyes and his mouth, put his nose down, and went low, into the gap where the shell's rim had lifted off the clay, under the edge of the oldest stone in the second tier, the way a dog goes under a porch.  
His head broke into air.  
It was a pocket no bigger than Ling Mei's sleeping platform, trapped beneath the curve of the shell, black as the inside of a closed fist and smelling of ten thousand years of turtle: moss, mud, cold, patience. Romeo hauled his front paws up onto a ridge of shell and hung there, coughing up lake, his ears plastered flat, his eye stinging, his tail streaming out behind him in the water like weed.  
Above, very slowly, the shell settled back down. The lake bed sighed. The water outside went still.

He felt the extra thread of spirit settle into his channels, thin and clean as spring water, and was too cold to be pleased about it.

*Three,* said Bao, after a while. The voice had shrunk back down to something that fit inside a skull, but it was awake now, really awake, sour and slow and unmistakable. *I shrugged a whole lake and I got three.*  
*You got three more than you had.*  
*I can feel a toe.* A pause, long enough for Romeo's teeth to start chattering. *One toe. Back left. It's cold. I'd forgotten my feet could be cold. I don't care for it.*  
*Welcome back,* said Romeo.  
*You barked at me.*  
*You told me to knock louder.*  
*I told you to knock. Like a civilised creature. Not to stand in a hole and scream at my shell like a fishwife.* Another pause. *It was very loud, Romeo. For something your size.*  
From Bao, that was nearly a speech of gratitude. Romeo's tail, still floating behind him, wagged in the water. He let it.  
*Who is it?* he asked. *On the shore.*  
The silence went on so long he thought the old turtle had fallen asleep again.  
*I don't know,* Bao said at last. *I've been asleep. But I know what it smells like, because I've been smelling it since the leaves fell. It smells like the chain, Romeo. The one that came down out of the clouds. I was a hundred li away, under water, and I smelled it then. It smells like that.*  
Romeo hung on the ridge of shell in the dark, and did not flinch, because his neck was under water and there was nothing to flinch from.  
*It knew I can't die,* he said.  
*Then it knows more than it ought to.* The shell around him shifted very faintly, the way a person shifts when they are thinking. *And it's patient. It'll come back next new moon and tie the three I broke, and then some.* Then, sharper: *And you've got somewhere to be. You smell of a person. A young one. And of fear that isn't yours.*  
*Her name is Ling Mei. There's a naming at the sect the morning after tomorrow. If she brings no dog, they'll know. And a collector is coming the day after that.*  
*Can you walk home in a day and a half?*  
*Just about. If nothing goes wrong.*  
*Something always goes wrong for you,* said Bao. *Your luck is a disgrace. I've said so for two thousand years.* A long, slow breath went through the stone. *There's a faster way down than the way you came. Water goes downhill quicker than dogs. You won't like it.*  
Romeo, who was soaked to the skin, bleeding into his eye, holding a sodden rice ball around his neck and clinging to the underside of a turtle at the bottom of a lake, considered how much more there was in the world for him to dislike.  
*Tell me,* he said.  
---

At that same hour, a day's hard climb below, Ling Mei sat on the floor of her hut with her back to the sleeping platform and her right hand pinned under her thigh, because it would not stop shaking.  
It had been bad since dusk. Around midnight it had become something else. It was as if the tide in her had felt something, far off and very large, turn over in its sleep, and had lurched toward it. For a moment the whole of her had leaned toward the mountain, toward nothing, and the floorboards had seemed to tilt, and she had heard, or thought she heard, a very small dog barking at the bottom of a very deep well.  
Then it was gone, and her hand was shaking again, and she was alone.  
"You'd better be all right," she told the third floorboard. "You'd better be stuffing yourself with sausage somewhere, you horrible old sock."  
The floorboard, which smelled convincingly of a sick dog, said nothing.  
Someone knocked on the door.  
Ling Mei went very still. It was not Zhao Peng's knock, which was a kick. It was polite and dry and had a ledger behind it.  
"Outer disciple Ling Mei," said the junior steward through the wood. "Elder Fang sends word. He's concerned for your dog's health, with the naming so close. The sect's beast-physician will come to examine it." A small, apologetic cough. "At first light."

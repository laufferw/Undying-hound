# **Chapter 14: Things That Are Mine**

For the space of one breath, nobody in the hut moved.

Ling Mei sat on the sleeping platform with a wet dachshund wrapped in a blanket on her lap and her face gone the colour of rice paper. The candle stood on the floor. The empty bowl stood beside it, licked clean. And between the bowl and the door, as plain as a mouth open in the middle of the floor, was the square black hole where the third floorboard should have been, breathing up a smell of bitterroot and cold earth and river.

⟨The board,⟩ Romeo said into her mind. ⟨Quietly. With your foot.⟩

She did not ask how. She put him down on the blanket, slid off the platform, hooked the floorboard with her toes where it lay against the wall and dragged it back across the hole. It dropped into place with a soft wooden clap. She pressed it down with her heel. It sat a little proud of its neighbours, the way it always did, because a dog had once chewed the edge.

"Disciple Ling?" said the voice outside. Patient. Not yet less patient.

"Coming, Elder. I'm not dressed. One moment."

It was a good lie. Romeo could smell it from where he lay, and it smelled almost exactly like the truth, because her robe was half off one shoulder from wrestling him out of the shaft. ⟨Listen to me,⟩ he told her, fast. ⟨Don't hide that I'm wet. Don't hide that I'm hurt. He'll smell both through the door. Hide the hole. Everything else, tell him the truth about a stupid dog.⟩

⟨What truth?⟩ she thought back.

⟨That I ran off and you found me in the drain.⟩

She looked at him. She smoothed her robe. She opened the door.

---

Elder Fang Wuji came in out of the night with snow on the shoulders of his grey robe and lamplight behind him, and the hut, which had seemed small with one girl and one dog in it, became very small indeed.

Behind him, Brow tried to follow. The great blue-white wolf got his head and one shoulder through the doorway and then simply stopped, wedged, filling the frame from post to lintel, his pale eyes level with the candle. Cold air poured in around him. He did not seem embarrassed. Bound wolves, in Romeo's long experience, were rarely embarrassed by anything; it had been trained out of them along with their names.

Fang Wuji did not look at the floor. He did not look at the bowl. He looked at the dog.

Romeo let him.

He lay on the blanket in exactly the shape that the day had left him: a long, damp, wretched tube of a dog, ears plastered flat, fur in wet red-and-white spikes, the split above his eye crusted dark, the scrapes along his flank showing pink where the gravel had taken the hair. He was still shivering, in long slow waves from his shoulders to the white tip of his tail, and he did not have to pretend a single shudder of it. He rolled a little onto his side, showing the white belly, which is what a small dog does when a large thing looks at it, and he made his eyes enormous, and he tilted his head up toward the elder with the face he used on cooks.

It was the face that said: *I am small and wet and blameless, and I believe you may have a sausage.*

"He ran off," said Ling Mei. "This afternoon. I was looking for him half the night. He was in the big drain under the east wall, in the water. He'd got himself stuck."

True, Romeo's nose told him. Every word, more or less. He had been in a drain. He had been stuck. The drain had simply been somewhat longer than she was implying, and inside a mountain.

Fang Wuji crouched. He did it without hurry and without a sound, the way very strong men do, and his face came down to Romeo's level, and his smell came with it: pine resin, old ink, cold air, and under those, faint, something Romeo had not caught through the door. Tiredness. Not the tiredness of one late night. The other kind, the kind that settles into a man over months.

"The physician tells me he is a sick dog," said the elder. "Bitterroot in his bedding. Loose bowels. A taste for running." His eyes moved over the scrapes, the eye, the shivering. "She did not say he was a stupid one."

"He's very stupid," said Ling Mei, with feeling.

⟨Thank you,⟩ said Romeo.

---

Then the elder's spiritual sense came down over him like a hand.

Romeo had felt it before, through floorboards and through bitterroot, a cold wide pressure searching the room. It was different with nothing in between. It was close and slow and precise, the attention of a Nascent Soul cultivator who had decided to take his time. It slid over the wet fur and along the long back and down each short leg. It touched the little coal of qi in his belly, the tiny Level 2 ember that the rice ball and the warming powder had blown back to life, and paused there.

Romeo did not hide it. There was nothing left to hide it with. The last of the bitterroot was rubbed into the walls of a shaft under the floor, and his body was too cold and too spent to fold its own qi away. So he did the only thing left to a creature with no hands and no strength: he let it be small. He let it be a dog's warmth, a sick dog's, a stray's. Something a sect might register in a ledger as *unawakened* and never think about again.

The hand moved on. Up his chest. Up to his throat.

To the blue cloth.

Fang Wuji's eyes narrowed very slightly. He reached out, with his actual hand, two long fingers, and touched the knot.

Everything in Romeo's body tried to happen at once. The flinch came up out of his spine like a fish out of black water: *chain, silver, sit*. His lips wanted to come back from his teeth. His neck wanted to twist away. His legs, all four short ridiculous legs, wanted to scrabble backward off the platform and into any hole there was.

He held it. He held it the way he had held it against the pin, with his whole three thousand years. The only thing that moved was his tail, which thumped once against the blanket, weakly, in what anyone would take for hopeful greeting.

It was not hopeful greeting. It was his tail screaming.

"What's this?" said Fang Wuji.

"My lunch cloth." Ling Mei's voice was steady. "He carries it. He won't let me take it off him. I think it smells of food."

The elder's fingers stayed on the knot. Romeo smelled him breathe in. Smelled him smell it: silk, lamp smoke, and the thin, bright, terrible smell that every bound beast in this sect carried faintly in its fur, and that Romeo now carried, faintly, at his throat.

Something crossed Fang Wuji's face. It was gone before Romeo could name it.

"Brow," said the elder, without turning his head.

---

The wolf in the doorway lowered his great head. Ling Mei, very wisely, stepped back.

Brow's nose came across the hut toward the platform the way a boat comes into a small harbour, slowly and filling everything. It stopped a hand's width from Romeo's face. Up close, the wolf smelled of snow and old straw and the same silver, much stronger, worn in, as much a part of him as his coat. His breath was warm. His eyes were pale and unreadable.

He smelled Romeo's muzzle, and the scar on it. He smelled the cut over the eye. He smelled the flank, the scrapes, the wet, and Romeo knew precisely what he was finding there: river water that had never seen daylight, lake ice, gravel from a seam no one in the sect knew existed, and at the edge of it all, faint as a word whispered in another room, the lamp-and-silk smell of the stranger.

Then Brow brought his nose to the blue cloth, and breathed in, long and slow, and held it.

Romeo looked up into the pale eyes and did not speak into the wolf's mind. He did not dare. He simply lay there, belly half up, a small damp dog under a very large nose, and waited, with infinite patience, the way he waited for food.

Brow breathed out. Snorted, once, softly, so that the candle flame bent. Lifted his head away. Turned, as much as a wolf wedged in a doorway can turn, and lay down across the threshold with his chin on his paws and his back to the dog, a picture of complete boredom.

And Romeo, whose nose was fragmented and half-blind and still the best nose on nine tiers of mountain, smelled something go up the binding between wolf and master. It was very faint. It was the smell a thing makes when it is said through a leash instead of a mouth. It said, as plainly as words: ⟨Dog. Only a dog. Smells of the kennels.⟩

It was a lie.

Romeo had smelled lies in the mouths of emperors. He had never in three thousand years smelled one told by a bound beast to the hand that held it.

*One of two,* Romeo thought, and did not look at the wolf. *One of two. And you finally decide to tell me something useful, and it's that.*

---

Fang Wuji straightened. For a long moment he stood looking down at Romeo with his hands folded into his sleeves, and Romeo could not smell what he was deciding. The elder's qi was folded too tightly. Only the tiredness leaked out, and something under it that might have been disappointment, or relief, or both, held very still.

"A pity," he said at last.

Ling Mei said nothing. Romeo approved of that.

"Last month," said the elder, to the candle as much as to her, "a fox stood in front of that stone. A small vixen of no account, bound to the third-tier merchant who sold us our winter rice. The stone took her name. Pretty name. It glowed with it most of an afternoon, and everybody clapped." He was quiet for a breath. "Two days later the merchant's wagon left without her. He told me she ran off. He smelled, Disciple Ling, very strongly of the money he'd been paid for her."

In the doorway, Brow's ears moved, once, and went still.

"I came tonight," said Fang Wuji, "because I wished to know what I have in my ledger. If he had been more than a dog, I would have wanted to know it before the stone did. Things that are mine, I keep." He looked at Romeo again, and the tired eyes were not unkind and not kind either. They were an owner's eyes. "But he's a dog. A wet, stupid, sickly dog. So he'll stand in front of the stone in the morning with every other beast on the list, and the stone will find nothing, and that will be the end of it."

"Yes, Elder."

"Dry him. Keep the cloth on him, if it keeps him quiet." He turned toward the door, and Brow, with a groan, unwedged himself and backed out into the snow. On the threshold Fang Wuji paused, the lamplight making a long shadow of him across the floor, across the slightly proud third floorboard, which he did not look at.

"One more thing. You may as well hear it tonight. The whole yard will by morning." He did not turn round. "The inspector from the third tier sent word at dusk. The one who collects. He was expected the day after tomorrow. He'll come over the pass at first light instead, and he's asked particularly to attend the naming."

The door closed behind him. Three steps in the snow, a wolf's soft weight beside them, and then nothing but the wind.

---

Ling Mei stood with her back against the door for a long time. Then she came and sat on the platform and pulled the blanket, and the dog in it, back into her lap, and held on with both hands, the bad one and the shaking one, which was shaking again.

"You said you'd explain," she said.

⟨I will,⟩ said Romeo into her mind, and thumped his tail once against her knee because he was too tired to do anything else. ⟨All of it. The lake. The turtle. The thing with the lamp. Why I'm wearing this.⟩ He put his chin on her wrist. ⟨But not tonight, because that wolf just lied to his master for me, and I don't know why, and the elder says I'm a dog and smells as if he hopes he's wrong, and none of that is the bad part.⟩

"What's the bad part?"

⟨The stranger in the mountain was walking its pins tonight,⟩ said Romeo. ⟨Checking its knots. Getting ready. And now the collector is a day early.⟩

She was quiet. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. Outside, very far off, high on the mountain, so faint that only a nose like his would have caught it on the wind, there was a smell of lamp smoke, going up.

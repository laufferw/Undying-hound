# **Chapter 4: Small Enough to Miss**

There are two things a dachshund does when danger comes down the stairs. The first is to bark at it. Romeo had been doing the first thing for three thousand years and it had worked surprisingly often.

He did the second thing.

He dropped. Belly to stone, legs folded under him, ears flat, chin on the floor, he went from a dog to a long low smear of shadow in the space of a heartbeat, and then he was moving backward out of the lamplight the way he had once backed out of a badger's sett with the badger still in it: quietly, steadily, and without ever turning his head away from the thing that could kill him.

Footsteps on the stairs. Slow, heavy, unhurried. A man who owned every stone he stepped on.

*The drain,* Romeo thought, and measured it with his nose. Forty paces down the hall, past twenty pens, across open floor. On his old legs he could have crossed it before the footsteps reached the bottom step. On these legs he would be halfway there, wet and red and very visible, when the lamp swung round.

*Not the drain,* said Grey. *Here. The gutter under my pen. Now, hole-dog.*

Romeo did not argue. He slid along the base of the pens, keeping under the lip of the stone curb where the lamplight did not reach, and found what Grey meant: a shallow trench cut into the floor at the back of the last pen, carrying waste water down toward the drain. It was a hand deep and a hand and a half wide, and it smelled of everything a kennel has ever produced.

It was, in short, the perfect size for a sausage.

He poured himself into it nose first, flattened until his ribs creaked, and lay still in the cold slime with Grey's great grey bulk between him and the hall. The old wolf shifted, lay down along the bars, and let one heavy paw dangle over the trench like a careless curtain.

The footsteps reached the bottom of the stairs.

---

Elder Fang Wuji was not what Romeo had expected, which was itself a warning.

He had expected a big man, the kind of elder who wore his cultivation like armour and filled a doorway on purpose. Fang Wuji was of middle height and slight, with a neat grey beard, a plain dark robe, and the soft, tidy hands of a scholar. He carried a lamp of his own. He did not look at the wolves. He walked down the hall the way a man walks through his own kitchen at night, and every wolf he passed lowered its head a little further onto its paws.

Behind him came a wolf Romeo had not seen before.

It was enormous, even beside the pony-sized beasts in the pens: blue-white, broad as a cart, with a pale stripe running from the bridge of its nose up over its brow like a vein of iron in rock. Its collar was not black iron but dark bronze, and the runes on it were finer and deeper than on the others. It moved without sound. Its nose moved all the time.

Romeo smelled it, and his Heavenly Nose, fragmented and half-blind as it was, told him three things at once. That this wolf was strong, a great deal stronger than he was. That it had not been allowed to choose anything for itself in a very long time. And that it was, right now, smelling him.

The big wolf stopped at the door of Grey's pen. Its nostrils widened. Its head turned, slowly, toward the trench.

Grey stood up, stretched, and kicked over his water trough.

It went with a clang that rang down the whole stone hall. A wave of water slopped across the floor of the pen, over the curb, into the trench, and down the length of Romeo's back, washing the ditch mud out of his coat and the dog smell along with it, and carrying all of it away toward the drain in a gurgling, stinking flood.

Several of the wolves flinched. The Elder turned, lifted his lamp, and looked at Grey with mild distaste.

"Old Nine," he said. His voice was soft and very clear. "Still clumsy."

Grey lowered his head and did not answer. In the trench, half-drowned and wholly furious, Romeo felt the cold water run in at one ear and out the other, and kept perfectly still. The great blue-white wolf breathed in once more, frowned, if a wolf can frown, and found nothing but spilled water and old kennel. It moved on.

Then the Elder's spiritual sense came.

Romeo felt it before anything else: a cool, thin pressure that spread out from the man like ripples across a pond, washing through the pens, the walls, the floor, the trench. Nascent Soul, at least. It touched every wolf and read every collar. It touched Romeo.

And passed over him.

Deep in his channels, the last of the afternoon's bitterroot still lay across his qi like a sheet of ice on a pond, the little sea he had gathered all day sluggish and dull beneath it. To the Elder's sense, the thing in the trench was a lump of cold meat in cold water. A drowned rat. Less.

---

Fang Wuji went into the lamplit chamber and set his lamp down beside the altar. From the trench, Romeo could see only a slice of him through the bars: his sleeve, his hand, the curve of the grey stone and its band of silver.

The Elder laid two fingers on the stone.

It hummed. Romeo felt it in his teeth. And then, very faintly, in a voice that was not a voice, like wind passing over the mouth of a jar, the stone spoke.

*Bright-Tail-Under-the-Bamboo.*

The fox's name. The one it had given itself, alone, the morning it woke up and knew it was someone.

The stone said it once, and then, more faintly, a second time, and then it was silent, and the silver band gave off a little breath of that cold, clean, patient scent that made every hair on Romeo's back try to stand up at once in a trench too shallow to let them.

"Kept clean," the Elder murmured, to the stone or to the wolf. "Good."

The great blue-white wolf sat down in the doorway, filling it.

"Eight days, Brow," said Fang Wuji. "When the collector comes, I want the ledger clean. Every beast on this mountain touched and named, and nothing unaccounted for." His fingers tapped the stone, once, twice. "Tomorrow we register the outer quarter. Strays. Chickens. Ducks, if they bring ducks. It will be a long, tedious morning, and somewhere in it, I expect, the girl's dog."

The wolf called Brow did not move. Its eyes were on the hall.

"The Zhao boy says it stole a pill off his belt," the Elder went on, in the same mild tone. "It did not steal it, of course. It tore his pouch open and dropped the pill at his feet, a stolen Crimson Marrow with Elder Qiu's seal on the wax, at the very moment a discipline deacon came round the corner." The fingers tapped again. "A dog does not know which pill is stolen, Brow. A dog does not know when the deacon is coming. A dog eats the pill."

A rat ran out from under the next pen.

It was a large rat, brown and slick, and it went along the curb at a busy trot, right past Romeo's nose, so close that its tail brushed his whiskers. Every single dachshund who had ever lived, back through the centuries of them, all the way back to the first small stubborn dog a farmer ever sent down a hole, rose up in Romeo at once and said, as one: *THAT.*

His back legs bunched. His jaws parted.

*Don't,* said Grey, very quietly.

Romeo did not. It cost him more than the lightning had. The rat trotted on, unbitten and unaware, and vanished down the drain, and somewhere inside him a three-thousand-year-old cultivator and a very young dog sat side by side and sulked.

In the chamber, the Elder took his fingers from the stone. He picked up his lamp. He came back down the hall at the same unhurried pace, Brow at his heels, and at the doorway where Romeo had stood five minutes before, he stopped.

He bent. He picked something up off the floor between two fingers and held it to the light.

It was a hair. Short, wet, and red, with a white tip.

Fang Wuji looked at it for a long moment. Then he wrapped it in a fold of paper from his sleeve, tucked it away, and climbed the stairs without another word. The door closed above them, and the lamp went with him, and the kennels were dark.

---

Romeo came out of the trench as a wet thing comes out of a river: slowly, and then all at once, in a shake that started at his nose and travelled down the whole length of him and finished in a spray from the white tip of his tail.

*You kicked your trough over on me,* he said.

*You're welcome,* said Grey. He had lain back down. In the dark, his torn ear was a ragged shape against the bars. *Brow would have had you. He smells better than any of us. They give him the best of everything.*

*Including the best collar.*

A long pause.

*Including that.*

Romeo looked up at the old wolf through the bars. He knew the smell coming off him now. He had smelled it on Ling Mei the first night, salt and tiredness, a long, long tiredness. On a wolf it smelled older.

*Why help me?*

Grey closed his eyes. *The fox,* he said. *It talked to me, the one night it was here. It was very young. It kept asking when its master was coming, and I kept telling it, you don't have one, little thing, that's the trouble.* He breathed out. *Go home, hole-dog. And whatever you do tomorrow, don't let that stone hear you.*

---

He came back up through the floor of the hut a little before midnight, filthy, half-frozen, and smelling worse than any dog in the history of the outer quarter, and Ling Mei, who had been sitting awake on the edge of the platform with her knees drawn up, took one breath and put her sleeve over her face.

"What did you *do*?"

*I hid in a sewer,* Romeo said, *under a wolf. It was an educational evening.*

She heated water on her little brazier and washed him in the bowl that caught the middle leak, and he let her, and did not complain, and she noticed that he did not complain, and her hands went gentler. While she worked he told her all of it. The stone. The fox's name. Brow. The hair.

When he got to the hair, she stopped scrubbing.

"He knows."

*He suspects. A man who suspects comes to look. A man who knows comes with a cage.* Romeo shook his ears. *He did not come with a cage.*

"Yet."

*Yet,* he agreed.

Afterward, because it was night and nights were for the tide, she lay down on her back and breathed, out first, all the way, and let the breath come back. He lay with his nose against her wrist and smelled the river in her arms push against its old banks. It was rougher tonight. Twice she gasped and her fingers clenched into the blanket, and once she rolled onto her side and was quietly sick into the third bowl, and then lay back down and went on breathing without being told. Romeo did not say anything. There are kinds of courage it is impolite to praise out loud.

"Two," she said hoarsely, with her eyes shut.

*Two,* said Romeo.

She was asleep within moments. He was not.

He lay in his den beneath her, curled on the thread of earth qi, and turned the problem over and over like a bone. The bitterroot hid qi. He had proved that tonight with his own wet hide. But the stone had not spoken the fox's strength or its realm or its level. It had spoken its *name*. And a name was not qi. A name was not something you could numb with a root a poor old man once chewed for toothache.

Romeo had a name. The one Old Man Shen had given him, which was a perfectly good name for a dog. And under it, older and quieter, the one he had given himself, three thousand and some years ago, on the morning he ate a fallen peach and woke up and knew he was someone.

He had not said that name aloud in a very long time.

He did not intend to let a rock say it for him.

---

The line outside the Beast Hall at the hour of the snake was long, loud, and smelled powerfully of chicken. There were outer disciples with cats, outer disciples with crickets in little cages, one boy with a goat that was eating his sleeve. Romeo rode inside the front of Ling Mei's robe with a chewed stalk of bitterroot going numb on his tongue, his ears over her sash and his qi lying still as fish under ice.

At the head of the line, at a table under the eaves, sat the junior steward with the ink-stained fingers, a register open in front of him. Beside him, on the terrace steps, lay Brow.

The steward looked up, ran his eyes down the line, and found Ling Mei. Something in his face apologized without meaning to.

"Outer disciple Ling Mei," he called. "By order of the Elder, you're not to wait. You and your dog are to come inside now."

He swallowed. "The Elder will be doing your assessment himself."

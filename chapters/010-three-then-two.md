# **Chapter 10: Three, Then Two**

The far side of the saddle was nothing like the near side.

The near side faced the wind and had been scoured to the bone. The far side was where the wind put everything it had stolen. It had been doing so all winter, and it had built a slope of snow so deep and so smooth that it shone under the stars like the flank of a sleeping horse, running down from the lip of the saddle almost to the black water's edge.

Romeo looked at it for a long moment from between his two stones.

*No,* he thought.

Deep snow was the one enemy he had never learned to fight. He had fought a tiger in the fourth tier and bitten it on the inside of the hind leg, where its striped fur thinned, and it had gone home and told no one. He had fought a man with a sword as long as a door. Deep snow could not be bitten. Deep snow simply received a dachshund, all of him, legs and belly and opinions, and closed over the top.

But the seam ran under it. He could smell it going down, clay and cold and that slow weight of earth qi, and beside it the pale, dry thread of silk that ran toward the lantern on the shore. If he tunnelled along the seam the whole way, it would take him until morning. He did not have until morning.

He had, he decided, one other way down.

He crept to the lip of the saddle, lay down on his belly with his front legs stretched out ahead of him and his hind legs stretched out behind, so that he was entirely one long line of red and white dog, and let the mountain have him.

He went down the slope like a dropped spoon.

It was very fast. It was very quiet, which was the point, and very cold, which he had expected, and it was also, to his horror, delightful. The snow hissed under his chest. The wind lifted his frozen ears and flapped them against his skull like two stiff little flags. Some ancient, idiot part of him, the part that had chased spirit squirrels and buried other dogs' bones for three thousand years, rose up in his chest and tried to come out of his mouth as a bark of joy.

*Absolutely not,* Romeo told it, and bit down on his own tongue.

He steered by his nose. When the seam-smell drifted left, he dropped his left shoulder and the snow carried him left; when the lantern smell grew too strong, he swung away from it. A third of the way down he hit a buried rock, went end over end twice, and came to rest upside down in a drift with his tail sticking out of it, wagging. He had not told it to wag. He made it stop, dug himself out backwards, lay down on his belly again and went on.

The slope flattened. The snow under him slowed and thickened and finally stopped him, gently, the way a hand stops a ball, perhaps forty paces from the shore and thirty from the lantern.

He lay there and did not breathe.

The figure in pale silk did not turn. Its hands went on moving, knot and feed, knot and feed, quick and patient. A new thread came over the snow from the west, pale as a hair, and the hands caught it without looking and wove it in with the others. Out across the ice the threads ran together into a single line, and the line went down into the open water and was gone.

Romeo put his nose into the snow, found the seam beneath it, and went to ground.

---

Here, at the bottom of the bowl, the snow was soft and the earth beneath it was not frozen all the way down. The lake had been breathing on this shore for longer than there had been sects, and the ground remembered it. Two forelegs down, the snow gave way to gravel. Three down, the gravel was damp. Five down, it was very nearly warm.

He dug the way his body had been built to dig, by the hunters of some country he had never seen who had wanted a dog that could go into the earth after a badger and come out again with the badger. Front paws scooping, scooping, chest pushing the loose gravel back under him, hind paws kicking it out behind in a steady patter. His long back, for once, was not a weakness. It was a shovel's handle. He went down along the seam into the bank like a needle going back into cloth.

When he had gone the length of three dogs, he stopped, curled into a ball in the dark with his nose under his tail, and shook.

It was a long time before the shaking stopped. Feeling came back into his paws as it always does, as fire. He licked them, one by one, and growled very quietly at each of them in turn, and when that did not help he did what Shen had taught him to do on a winter night in a ditch with no roof three thousand years ago. He let the warmth come to his nose. The earth qi of the seam was slow and heavy and old, and it did not mind being breathed. He drew it in, and it went down into his narrow channels and pooled in his belly like broth.

*Ancient,* Romeo said. *Yes. I could have told you that.*

The System did not reply, but the panel lingered a little longer than it needed to, the way an old servant lingers in a doorway.

---

He did not dig straight at the water. That was the obvious thing, and he had not survived three thousand years by doing the obvious thing in the presence of something that smelled of nothing. Instead he followed the seam, because the seam knew where it was going, and it went down and under the shore and out beneath the lake bed, through gravel and then through clay and then, at last, to stone.

His claws rang on it. He stopped at once and listened.

Nothing above him changed. The lake pressed down on the clay over his head with all its black, cold weight, and somewhere on the shore behind him the lantern burned, and the hands knotted, and the threads went down. He could smell the nearest of them now. It ran through the water perhaps a body's length above him, through the dark, sinking at a slant toward the middle of the lake, and it smelled like the pins: silk and silver and something underneath both that made the hair stand up along his back. *Silver (bound),* his nose told him. *Source: unknown.*

He did not touch it. He was not a fool. He had a neck.

The stone beneath his paws was smooth and very old and slightly curved. He put his nose to it and breathed in, and it was not lake bed at all.

Two thousand years ago, on a hot afternoon in the second tier, Old Turtle Bao had been sunning himself on a mud bank with his head out, his eyes closed, and his mouth slightly open, which was how he had spent most of the third century. Romeo, at that time a Core Formation dog of some reputation, had been lying beside him in the shade of his shell, chewing a stick.

"If you ever need to wake me," Bao had said, without opening his eyes, which took him most of the afternoon, "don't shout at the water. Water lies. It carries everything and keeps nothing. Knock on the stone."

"You're asleep for a hundred years at a time," Romeo had said. "How would you even hear a knock?"

"I'm a turtle," Bao had said. "I have been listening to stone since before your ancestors decided to have short legs. Three knocks, then two. Like a heron walking. Anything else I'll assume is an earthquake, and I sleep through those."

"And if it's the end of the world?"

"Then knock louder," Bao had said, and gone back to sleep for a month.

Romeo had not thought of that afternoon in a thousand years. He thought of it now, lying in the dark under the lake with his chest pressed to the edge of an ancient shell, and something in his throat tightened that had nothing to do with the cold.

*You might have mentioned,* he thought, *that I'd need a hammer.*

He had no hammer. He had no hands. He had four paws whose claws were, at best, a little longer than they should be because Ling Mei had not yet worked out how to trim them, and a head, and a jaw, and a tail. The tail was too soft. The paws would scratch, not knock.

He backed up as far as his tunnel let him, which was not far. He lowered his head. He fixed in his mind, very clearly, the spot on the stone in front of him.

And he butted it.

The knock went into his skull and out the other side and rang in his teeth. Stars that had nothing to do with the sky bloomed behind his eyes. He swayed, set his feet, backed up again, and did it again. And a third time, his ears flopping forward over his eyes with each blow.

Then he waited, counting the pause the way a heron pauses between steps. One. Two.

Twice more. Hard. The second one split the skin above his eye, and he felt it bleed, warm and then cold.

Three, then two.

He lay down with his chin on the stone and waited.

---

For a long time there was nothing. The lake pressed down. The thread a body's length above him trembled faintly as another knot was tied on the shore and the line drew tighter. He became aware, in the way you become aware of a sound you have been hearing for an hour, that every one of the threads was drawing tighter. Not quickly. With great patience. The way a noose tightens on a sleeping thing so as not to wake it.

Romeo raised his head to butt the stone again, louder, as he had been told.

Before he could, the stone moved.

It was not much. It was a shiver, the smallest possible shift, the movement of something so large that its smallest movement was the size of a hut. Gravel trickled down the walls of his tunnel. The clay overhead groaned. And through the stone, through his chin and his jaw and the bones of his skull, came a sound he had not heard in a thousand years.

One knock. Very slow. From very, very far down.

Then a voice. Not in the water. Not in his ears. In the bone.

*...Is it,* said Old Turtle Bao, and stopped, and the stop lasted the length of a long breath, *...the end of the world?*

Romeo found his tail was wagging. He let it.

*Close enough,* he said.

Another long pause. The stone under his chin warmed very slightly, the way a stone warms when something has been lying on it, very still, for a very long time.

*Romeo,* said Bao. *You're... small.*

*I noticed. Listen to me, old shell. Someone on the shore is—*

*Tying my feet.* The words came slowly, but they came without surprise, and that was worse than anything else. *Yes. I thought it was a dream. I have been dreaming of silk since the leaves fell. A little more every new moon.* A pause, deep as the lake. *Tonight is the new moon.*

*Then wake up properly and swim away.*

*I can't feel my feet,* said the oldest creature Romeo had ever loved, very simply. *I haven't been able to feel my feet for some time.*

Above them, on the shore, the quick hands stopped.

Romeo felt it through the thread before he understood it: the line above his head, which had trembled with every knot for an hour, went still. Then it went slack. Then, very gently, as if someone on the far end had laid one fingertip on it, it was plucked, once, like the string of a zither.

The note ran down the thread into the lake. It ran past him in the dark. And at the far end of it, at the bottom, in the middle, where the smell of old shell was strongest, he heard Bao's slow thought falter, as a sleeper's breath falters on the edge of a dream.

Then, far behind him, at the mouth of his tunnel, the dark turned yellow and soft. Lantern light. It lay across the gravel he had kicked out behind him, across his tracks, across the long dent in the drift where a small dog had come down the mountain on his belly.

"Hush," said the voice on the shore.

And this time Romeo was almost certain it was not meant for the water.

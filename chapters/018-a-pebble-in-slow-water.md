# **Chapter 18: A Pebble in Slow Water**

The stone reached for him.

It did not move. Nothing about it moved. But Romeo had been touched by a great many things in three thousand years, by hands and claws and lightning and the cold fingers of qi deviations, and he knew the feeling of being reached for. It came up out of the grey rock like the draught from under a door. It went past his fur and his skin and the small hot ember of his Qi Gathering, past the dirt and the snow-damp and the smell of a dog who has slept two hours in a blanket, and it went down, straight down, to the bottom of him.

To the place he turned toward when he was called.

The silver band brightened. Romeo, from his cliff-foot view, saw the light come up pale as frost, and in it, very fast, something ran like water over stones: lines and hooks and dots, a script with too many strokes in it, a script nobody in this yard had been taught because nobody had written it for two thousand years. It did not say *Romeo*. It was beginning to say something underneath *Romeo*, something a small red puppy had once called himself in the dark, before an old man found him on a riverbank and gave him a better one.

*No,* thought Romeo. *That one isn't for you.*

It did not care what he wanted. It was a stone. It had been made to take, and it was taking, and the collector was crouched an arm's length away with his bare hand still held out and the slits of his mask fixed on the band, and the collector was close enough to read.

---

There is a thing that every dog knows, and that Romeo had spent the better part of three thousand years trying to rise above, which is that you cannot come to two voices at once.

The collector had called him in Old Man Shen's way. The first sound long, the last one lifting. It was a key cut to an old lock, and the old lock had opened, and everything behind it was standing in the doorway blinking. The river. The golden afternoon. The old man with his trousers rolled up, calling and calling and never once losing patience. The name under the name.

But Brow had stood here not an hour ago with a name full of snow and a fox's name besides, and he had given the stone only the one his master used. Not the deepest. The nearest.

*Wear a little of what you hate,* Bao had said. *Collectors look for what is missing.*

Romeo did not look at the collector's hand. He did not look at the band. He turned his head, which was the only part of him not frozen to the snow, and looked back across three steps of trampled yard at a girl in a grey robe with one hand that would not straighten and one that would not stop shaking.

She was not calling him. She had been told to step back and she had stepped back, exactly three steps, and her mouth was shut tight. But Romeo did not need her to call. He had heard it in every time she had said his name since the day she split her last bun with a stray, and he had heard her say it this morning in front of the whole sect: *It's the one he comes to.*

*Yes,* thought Romeo. *It is.*

He let it be true. That was all. He did not push qi anywhere or seal anything or do any of the clever things a Tribulation Transcendence cultivator might have done if he had had a Tribulation Transcendence cultivator's meridians. He was a Qi Gathering dog with a running nose, and he did the only thing a dog can really do, which is decide who he belongs to.

His tail decided first. It swung, once, hard, toward Ling Mei.

The water-script in the band stuttered. The old hooked strokes thinned and went under, like a stone sinking, and the light smoothed over them and settled, plain and modern and a little crooked, the way a deacon writes in a ledger when his fingers are cold.

*Romeo.*

Then the stone took its sip.

He felt it go. It was not large. It was not anything a man watching would have seen. It was the feeling of a warm room with one shutter opened somewhere at the back of the house: a thinness, a draught. Romeo reached, out of habit, for the river afternoon, and it was there, all of it, the gold light and the old man's voice. Only the smell of the water was a little further off than it had been this morning. As if he were remembering a memory of it, and not the thing.

The light faded from the band. In the yard, a deacon's brush scratched. Somebody coughed.

And Romeo, who had been holding his breath for longer than a small dog should, let it out all at once through a nose that had been running for two days, directly onto the collector's bare and outstretched hand.

It was a very large sneeze. It was a sneeze with three thousand years of dignity behind it, and a good deal of snow, and it went everywhere.

---

The yard did not laugh at once. It waited, the way a yard waits to see whether a thing is permitted. Then someone in the second row made a noise like a kettle, and someone else lost the fight beside them, and in a moment the outer disciples of the Azure Wolf Sect were laughing the cold, helpless, shoulder-shaking laugh of people who had been afraid all morning and had just watched a sick dog sneeze on a Hound-Catcher.

The collector did not move his hand. He looked at it. Then he drew a handkerchief from his sleeve, pale grey like everything else about him, and wiped his fingers clean, one at a time, in the same unhurried order he had taken his glove off.

"He doesn't come to strangers," he said. His voice was as light and pleasant as ever. "The disciple did tell me."

"He's a dog, Inspector," said Fang Wuji from the steps, in a tone that closed the subject like a lid.

"Yes." The collector drew his glove back on. He stayed crouched a moment longer, the mouthless mask level with Romeo's face, and Romeo smelled him properly for the first time, now that the bare skin was covered: lacquer, horse, frost, silver, and under it all, faint as the inside of a sleeve, silk and lamp smoke. *You've stood very close to something,* Romeo thought, *or something has stood very close to you.* He could not tell which. He could not smell any lie on the man, because the man had not yet told one.

Then the collector said, so quietly that the laughter in the yard covered it, so quietly that only a nose on the snow an arm's length away could have caught the shape of it:

"Good dog."

And stood, and turned away.

Romeo did not move. He had said those two words himself, not an hour ago, about a wolf who had kept his secrets in front of the stone. He had meant them with every year he had. He did not know what the collector meant by them, and that was worse than any threat could have been.

---

The ceremony ended the way such things end, with a great deal of standing about. The deacons conferred. The stone was not loaded back on its sledge but left where it was, in the middle of the yard, with two of the collector's masked escorts beside it in the snow.

"Elder Fang." The collector had remounted. From the saddle he looked down at the steps, at the elder, at the great blue-white wolf lying at the elder's knee. "Your ledger is in good order. I find nothing in it to hurry for." A pause, just long enough. "I will stay a little, all the same. The passes are bad, and the moon is thin. I should like to see your beasts again when it has gone dark. Six nights, I think."

Fang Wuji's face did not change. "The sect is honoured."

"And I'll leave you a gift." A gloved hand lifted, and the escort by the riderless horse lifted the cage down onto the snow at the foot of the steps. The fox inside did not stir. "She was bred in your hills. She ought to finish in them. She eats very little. I doubt she'll be any trouble."

Romeo, from Ling Mei's arms (she had come forward the moment the stone went dark and scooped him up, blanket and snow and all, and had not let go since) watched Brow. The wolf did not get up. He did not look at the cage. He lay with his chin on his paws and his pale eyes on nothing, and from twenty paces away Romeo smelled the binding between him and Fang Wuji go so tight it sang.

*Bait,* thought Romeo. *That isn't a gift. That's a hook with a fox on it.*

It took Ling Mei most of the afternoon to stop shaking, and she did not let him see any of it, which meant she did it with her back to him while she built up the brazier with charcoal she was not supposed to have.

Romeo lay on the floor in front of it, because the sleeping platform was a special case and he did not have the strength today to argue with it. The heat came into his damp coat a little at a time. His long back ached from the cold, all along the length of it, the way it did now whenever he forgot he was not the dog he had been. His throat was raw. His shoulder hurt. He was alive, and registered, and named, and every one of those things felt slightly less certain than it had at dawn.

"It said Romeo," said Ling Mei, to the brazier.

⟨It did.⟩

"Before that. It started to say something else. I saw it. The light went strange." She turned round at last. Her eyes were red. "What was it going to say?"

He thought about lying to her. He had lied to emperors. It would have been very easy.

⟨The name I had before Shen,⟩ he said. ⟨The one I gave myself. Nobody has heard it since I was a puppy the first time. Not even Bao.⟩

"And it nearly took it."

⟨It took a little of the other one instead. Not much.⟩ He put his chin down on his paws, and thought about the river, and the smell of the water that was now one step further off. ⟨It's all right. I have a great deal of it.⟩

She sat down beside him on the cold floor, which outer disciples were not supposed to do either, and put her good hand flat on his side, over the ribs, and left it there.

"How did you stop it?"

Romeo considered several answers, most of them clever. Then he gave her the true one.

⟨I looked at you.⟩

Ling Mei did not say anything for a long time. Then she said, "You horrible old sock," in a voice that did not sound anything like the way she usually said it, and scratched him, very carefully, behind one ear.

His tail thumped the floorboards twice. He let it.

---

He went down into the den after dark, because the earth-qi trough was the only thing that ever really warmed him through, and because a dog who has been looked at by a whole yard all day wants, more than anything, to be underground.

The third floorboard sat a little proud, as it always did. The shaft was cold. The round chamber smelled of him and of Ling Mei and of the faint green breath of the vein. He lay down in the trough and let the earth come up into him slowly, and for a few minutes he did not think about stones or names or mouthless masks or a tall narrow thing walking up toward a lake where an old turtle sat alone with one toe and twenty-four threads, and six nights left before somebody tied them all again.

Then the back-door tunnel moved.

It was not a sound. It was a smell, sliding in along the earth from the ditch end: snow, reeds, and something long and low and quick and brown that smelled of an old man's sleeve.

Every hair on Romeo's back went up. Something very old and very simple woke in his chest and said, perfectly reasonably, *That. In my tunnel. In my den. Now.*

*Sit,* he told himself, and hated the word, and sat.

Two bright eyes looked at him out of the dark of the back door. A narrow head. Whiskers. The stoat called Smoke came one length into the chamber and stopped, just out of reach, with the air of a creature that knew to the inch exactly how long a dachshund was.

Then a voice spoke in Romeo's mind, small and dry and very tired, like a twig breaking under snow.

⟨The old man says,⟩ said the stoat, ⟨the stone was warm after you.⟩

Romeo did not move.

⟨He says since it came down from the third tier he has touched it after every beast, and it has never once been warm.⟩ The whiskers twitched. ⟨He says he has an idea where the names go, and he would rather show you than say it. Come to the kennels by the drain at the hour of the rat. Before the masks at the stone change their guard.⟩

The stoat began to back away into the tunnel. At the edge of the dark, it stopped.

⟨He also says,⟩ it added, ⟨please do not eat me. I am only the messenger, and I am quite old.⟩

# **Chapter 8: Stop Trying to Be Tall**

He drank in the end. A dog who will not drink because something frightened him at the water is a dog who will be frightened and thirsty, and Romeo had been both before and preferred to be only one at a time.  
He drank from the edge of the spring, well away from the pressed moss, and while he drank he read the place with his nose the way a scholar reads a page he suspects of lying.  
The knees had been there for some time. Long enough for the moss to give up and stay flat. The stranger had not eaten here, had not relieved themselves, had not so much as broken a twig. There was no sweat in the scent at all, which was odd, because the slope up from the sect wall was steep enough to make a strong man breathe hard, and a person who breathes hard sweats, even in the hour of the ox, even in late autumn. This one had climbed it and knelt in the cold and smelled, afterward, of nothing but clean silk and lamp smoke and that thin, cold, metallic thread running under both.  
And there was something else. Something small, at the lip of the spring, where the water came up out of the roots.  
Romeo put his nose down to it and stopped breathing.  
It was a pin. Silver, no longer than a girl's finger, pushed straight down into the wet clay where the seam broke the surface, so that only its head showed: a little bead of metal no bigger than a grain of rice, with a knot of pale silk thread tied around it. The thread ran a hand's width across the moss and vanished into the ground.  
He did not touch it. He breathed beside it, very carefully, and let the smell come to his nose.  
The water above the pin smelled the way it had smelled all the way up from the ditch: clay, cold, earth qi, the faint far drift of lake mud and old shell. The water below it smelled the same, but quieter. As if it were flowing past something that was holding it by the scruff. Not stopping it. Only holding it, so that it knew it was held.  
*Someone,* thought Romeo, *is putting a leash on a stream.*  
He sat in the dark with that for a long moment. He could pull the pin. His teeth were small, but they were sharp and they were his, and a silver pin was a silver pin. But the thread was taut, he could smell the tension in the silk, the way you can smell a bowstring, and a thing that is pulled tight at one end tends to notice when it goes slack at the other.

---

He found the second pin just before dawn, at a place where the seam came up as a wet black stain on a rock face, and the third a little after, where it ran shallow under a bed of pine needles. Each had its knot of silk. Each made the water below it smell a little quieter than the water above. Each had a pair of knee-prints beside it, and no sweat, and no food, and a line of tracks going on uphill.  
The tracks themselves bothered him almost as much as the pins. They were the prints of soft-soled boots, a narrow foot, a long stride. A tall person. And they were shallow. On frozen ground that was no surprise, but where the path crossed a patch of damp clay by the third spring, a person of that height should have sunk to the welt of the boot, and this one had barely marked it. As though they weighed what a heron weighs. As though they had been walking for a long time, carefully, on a great many things that would break.  
At sunrise he stopped under a stand of young pines, untied the slip-knot of Ling Mei's blue cloth with one paw, and ate a rice ball.  
He meant to eat half of it. He was a cultivator of the Tribulation Transcendence realm, or had been, and had once fasted forty days on the summit of Mount Gaoqian to prove a point to a phoenix. He ate all of it in three bites and then stood looking at the remaining three with his head tilted, and the white tip of his tail moving slowly from side to side as if it had opinions of its own.  
*No,* he told the tail, and tied the bundle up again. It took four tries. Knots were not a thing paws were made for, and he had to hold one end in his teeth and push the other through with his nose, which put salt and rice and blue cotton up his nostrils and made him sneeze so violently that he sat down.  
Three left. The morning of Day 5\. The panel, when he called it, said **\[2 days, 1 hour\]**, and he did the sum again, the same sum, two days up and one day down, and it came out exactly as tight as it had come out in the hut, minus the hours he had spent smelling pins.  
Above the pines, the snow began.  
---

It did not begin politely. On the lower slopes there had been frost and then a crust of old white in the shadows, nothing a dog could not walk across. Then the ground lifted into a long open shoulder of mountain, and the snow lay over it from one side to the other, smooth and blinding under a white sun, and when Romeo set his first paw on it, his leg went in to the elbow.  
He pulled it out. He set the other one down. That went in to the elbow too, and then, because a dachshund's legs are short and his chest is deep and low and his belly is not far behind it, his chest went in after his legs, and then his belly, and he was lying in the snow like a loaf of bread set down in flour, with all four feet dangling in nothing.

*Yes,* said Romeo. *Thank you.*  
He tried bounding. A wolf would have bounded, and for about twenty paces he did too, launching his front half up out of the snow and letting it fall forward and dragging his back half after it, like a caterpillar that had been badly advised. By the twentieth bound his heart was going like a drum at a funeral and his ears were caked white to the tips and he had travelled, by his own careful estimate, a distance he could have rolled downhill in the time it took to blink.  
The stranger's tracks went on across the shoulder of the mountain, light and even. They barely broke the crust.  
Romeo lay in the trough he had made and panted and looked up at the sky, and the sky, white and indifferent, looked back.  
---

It had been snowing the winter Old Man Shen took him down to Willow Ford to sell ginseng. Romeo had been small then, even for a small dog. Not yet a cultivator. Not yet anything except a red-and-white pup with too much body and not enough leg, who had eaten a peach he should not have eaten, and had started, lately, to understand what people said.  
Halfway down the hill the snow had come up to Shen's knees. Romeo had tried to follow in the old man's tracks and fallen into each one like a stone into a well, and after the fourth time he had stood in the bottom of a footprint with only his ears showing and howled his small furious howl at the injustice of the world.  
Shen had stopped. He had looked down into the footprint for a long moment, and then laughed so hard he had to lean on his staff.  
"You keep trying to walk on top of it," the old man said. "Like a tall dog. Like a goat. Look at yourself." He crouched, and his dry hand came down and pressed on Romeo's back, gently, until the pup's belly was flat against the snow. "You weren't made to go over things, little Romeo. You were made to go under them. When the snow is too deep for your legs, stop trying to be tall."  
Then he had picked the pup up and put him inside his coat, against the warm of his ribs, and carried him all the way to Willow Ford anyway, because he was a soft old fool and had never once let a lesson get in the way of being kind.  
Romeo had not thought of that day in perhaps a thousand years.  
---

He stopped trying to be tall.  
He put his nose down into the snow, and then his head, and then he dug. Not across the surface, the way he had been floundering, but down and forward at a slant, his front paws going like the paddles of a river boat, throwing the white back between his hind legs in two plumes. A hand's depth down, the powder gave way to older, denser snow, packed by a month of wind. It held its shape. He could make a tunnel in it, low and narrow, exactly as wide as one long red dog, and the roof would stay.  
And there, a little deeper, under the old snow, was the seam.  
He could smell it through the white the way you can smell bread through a cloth: clay and earth qi and water, running beneath the snowfield in its own slow channel. The snow above it was softer. Warmer. Not by much, not enough for a human hand to tell, but a dachshund's nose was not a human hand, and the earth qi rising out of the seam had been loosening the snow over it all autumn, so that there ran beneath the whole shoulder of the mountain a faint, damp, yielding line, like the seam of a garment where the stitching has been let out.  
Romeo put his nose on it and went to ground.  
It was not fast. It would never be fast. But it was steady, and it was his, and it was the first thing that had felt right since he climbed out of the hut. In the blue dark under the snow there was no wind and no glare and no sky to look indifferent at him. There was only the cold smell of the seam ahead, and the sound of his own paws, and every so often, when he poked his head up through the crust to check his line, the stranger's shallow tracks running on above him, parallel, like a road he had chosen not to take.  
Once, about midday, he came up under a pin. He smelled it before he reached it, the silver like a cold coin on the tongue, and he stopped a body's length short and lay in his tunnel and listened. The thread ran down past his nose into the seam. The water beneath it ran quieter. Someone had knelt above him in the snow and pushed it down through the white with what must have been a very long, very steady arm.  
He dug around it, wide, the way you go around a sleeping snake.

Fifty-two. He had hoped for more. He ate a second rice ball at the hour of the goat, lying in his tunnel with the snow glowing blue around him, and this time he managed to save a corner of it for ten whole breaths before he ate that too.  
Two left.  
---

By late afternoon the light in the tunnel had gone from blue to grey, and the snow had begun to get into places on a dog that snow has no business going. His ears were stiff. His paws had stopped hurting, which he knew from long experience was worse than when they hurt. When he surfaced, the shoulder of the mountain was behind him, and ahead the slope ran up toward a high, narrow saddle between two peaks, and above the saddle the sky had gone the colour of a fish's belly.  
Somewhere past that saddle was the lake. He could smell it properly now, not a thin drift but a weight on the wind: deep cold water, black mud, moss so old it had forgotten it was green. And old shell, very faint. Very still.  
*Still asleep, old friend,* he thought. *Good. Stay asleep a little longer. There is someone on the stairs.*  
He went back under, and dug.  
It was near dusk, perhaps a li below the saddle, that he smelled the lamp.  
Lamp smoke first, warm and greasy and close, and then clean silk, and then, under both, so near it made his teeth ache, silver. He froze in his tunnel. Above him, a little ahead, a light was moving. He could see it through the snow, a soft yellow bloom in the grey roof of his burrow, swaying a little, as a lantern sways in a hand.  
It came closer. He heard the footsteps now, the faint compressed creak of soft boots on snow, far too light for the height of the stride. He heard them come down the slope toward him, along the line of the seam, along the line of his tunnel, slow and unhurried and exact.  
He did not breathe. He did not move. He made himself what he had made himself under the Beast Hall floor, under Fang Wuji's spiritual sense, under a hundred other things across three thousand years: small enough to miss.  
The footsteps stopped directly above him.  
The lantern light lay on the snow over his back, close enough that the roof of the tunnel glowed gold. Through a hand's depth of white, Romeo could smell that the person standing there did not sweat, and did not hurry, and was not surprised.  
Then, very gently, something long and thin and silver began to push down through the snow toward the seam, a finger's width from his nose.

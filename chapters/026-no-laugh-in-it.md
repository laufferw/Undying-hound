# **Chapter 26: No Laugh in It**

Romeo went back the way he had come, and he did not go carefully.

He went along the foot of the wall with his belly in the snow, down into the ditch, and along the trench he had ploughed with his own chest, faster than a dog with a strained back had any business going. His shoulder told him so. His hip told him so. His back opened its mouth to make another of its private remarks, and he did not stop to hear it. He pushed his nose into the reeds, found the low dark mouth of the back-door tunnel by the smell of himself, and went in.

The earth closed around him like a held breath. Forty paces. Level. His.

*Ling Mei,* he said as he ran. *Are you still sitting on it?*

A whisper, so faint it barely reached the floor: "Yes."

*Stay there. Something is in the den. It is underneath you. It will try to make you get up.*

"How?"

*By being someone you want to see.* He thought fast, the way he used to think in the old days with a sword coming down. *Listen to me. I do not speak out loud. I bark. I have never once said a word to you with my mouth. If you hear my voice in the air, it is not me.*

Silence over his head; then the creak of a board, and of a girl settling her weight more firmly, the way you would sit on the lid of a pot that was trying to boil over.

*Good girl,* he did not say, because she would have hit him.

---

Twenty paces from the end of the tunnel he slowed. At ten he stopped, lay flat, and read the dark ahead.

The round chamber smelled the way it always did: turned earth, the trough's mineral cold, the old physician's herbs, stoat (a week at least, as predicted), Ling Mei through the floor above. And in the middle of it, filling it from floor to roof the way water fills a jar, a hole.

It was bigger in here. In the open yard it had been a gap in the wind. In the dachshund-sized dark of his den it was a shape, and his nose drew it for him whether he wanted it or not. It was tall. Much too tall. The chamber was round and low, dug by a dog for a dog, and the thing that had come down the seam tunnel did not fit in it. It stood in the only place it could stand, at the bottom of the shaft, with whatever it had for a body going straight up into the dark like smoke up a chimney. And at the top of the shaft, flat against the underside of the third floorboard, exactly where Ling Mei was sitting, there was the absence of a hand.

It wasn't pushing. It was only resting there, waiting for the board to lift.

Romeo crept forward on his elbows until his nose was at the mouth of the tunnel. He did not go into the chamber. A terrier goes into the chamber. A dachshund lies in the earth at the mouth of the chamber and lets the quarry decide how much trouble it wants.

Something turned at the bottom of the shaft. Not a head; there was no head to smell. But the hole leaned toward him, and the cold in the den shifted.

"There you are," said Old Man Shen.

---

Romeo had prepared for it. He had told Ling Mei not to answer a beloved voice, and he had told himself the same thing, and he had already heard a voice come out of an empty slot under a lake. None of that helped. It was Shen's voice as it had been on good evenings, low and amused, roughened by woodsmoke, with the small whistle in it from the missing tooth on the left side. Three thousand years fell off Romeo like water off a shaken coat, and his tail moved against the tunnel floor, once, before he could stop it.

"Come out of there, little one," said the voice. "It's cold. Let it come to your nose."

*It is coming,* Romeo thought. *That's the trouble.* He smelled nothing. Shen had never once in his life spoken without smelling of onions.

"Romeo," said the voice, and it said the name the river way, the hat way, with the warm fall on the second half that no one alive had heard. Something in his chest pulled toward it like a hook in a fish's lip. He thought of the other name, the one under the coat, and held it closed in his teeth, and said nothing.

The hole waited. Then it said, gently, the way you would speak to a puppy who had wandered too near the cooking fire:

"Sit."

Every muscle in Romeo's body tried to obey.

It was not his choice. It was older than choice. The word went into him the way it had gone into him three thousand years ago on the threshold of heaven, with a silver chain coming down out of the cloud, and his haunches dropped half an inch toward the earth, and the flinch went through him from ears to tail as if something were closing around his throat from above.

And in the middle of the flinch, he noticed something.

Shen had said "sit" to him a thousand times. He had said it on hill paths and in doorways and in fields where vipers might sleep. And every single time, at the end of the word, there had been a small breath of a laugh, because Shen had never in his life been able to give an order to a dog without finding it slightly funny.

This one had no laugh in it.

He had heard a "Sit" with no laugh in it exactly once before.

Romeo stopped trying not to obey. He simply didn't. He laid his chin down on his paws in the mouth of his tunnel, which is not sitting, which is the opposite of sitting, which is what a dachshund does at the mouth of a badger's sett when it intends to stay there until the end of the world.

*You have his voice,* he said into the dark, sending it at the hole the way he would send to Bao, with no idea whether anything was there to hear. *You don't have his laugh.*

---

The hole was quiet a moment. Then it tried the other way.

Up the shaft, in the dark under the floorboard, a voice spoke aloud. It was dry and old and a little pompous, with long patient vowels, and Romeo, who had never heard it from the outside, needed a moment to understand that it was his own.

"Ling Mei," it said. "It's gone. I'm hurt. Lift the board."

Romeo did not breathe. Above him, a girl who had barely slept, who was terrified, who loved him, sat on a board in the dark and heard her dog asking her for help.

She didn't move. She didn't answer. After a long moment, very faintly, he heard her sniff. It was the particular sniff of a person keeping to herself an opinion about how much that voice sounded like an old man pretending to be wise.

*Good,* he thought, and found that his tail was wagging again, and let it.

The hole at the bottom of the shaft turned back toward him. The cold deepened. Frost crept across the lip of the trough. And Romeo heard the tap now, close: a stick on stone, a pause, and the slow drag of a right heel, as the thing that weighed nothing shifted its borrowed feet.

That was when he went in.

---

He did not go high. Everything in the world that had ever fought a dog expected it to come at the throat. Romeo went the way a badger-hound goes, flat and low and under, belly on the floor of his own den, between where the feet ought to be. They were not there. His bruised shoulder passed through something like the draught under a winter door. But he knew, better than he knew anything in the world, where that right heel would be. He had walked behind it for years. He knew the exact place it dragged, and the half-beat it lagged behind the left, and the little twist the old knee gave on frozen ground.

He bit there.

His teeth closed on cold and nothing, and then on something that was not nothing at all: something thin and tough and real, which caught between his back teeth the way a straw catches in a dog's mouth after he has rolled in the hay. He set his jaw and pulled, the way he had pulled a thousand roots out of a thousand hillsides, all four short legs braced, his long back screaming, and a thing that had been very carefully put on came, a little, undone.

The tapping stopped. The dragging heel stopped. For one heartbeat the den was full of a smell he had not smelled in three thousand years: onions, and pine sap, and an old cotton jacket, and river water, all of it at once, so strong it made his eyes run.

Then it was gone, and the hole went up and back and away from him, not walking now, not tapping, simply draining up the seam tunnel the way water drains out of a footprint. As it went, a voice came back down after it. Shen's voice still, though it had begun to fray at the edges like a coat with a seam pulled out.

"Dark of the moon, little one. The rest of him is up the hill. Come and fetch it."

Then nothing. Not even the hole.

Romeo lay on the floor of his den with his back on fire and opened his mouth. It was a scrap of thread no longer than his ear was wide, faded almost to brown but red at the core, cotton, twisted by hand. He knew it. He had watched an old man sit in a doorway, squinting and swearing, and stitch it into the brim of a straw hat that a young dog had carried out of a river.

He put his nose on it and did not move for a long time.

"Romeo?" Ling Mei whispered at last. "Is that you breathing?"

*Yes. It's gone. You can get up.*

"How do I know it's you?"

*Because I'm the one who isn't talking out loud,* he said. *And because your bottom must be frozen.*

There was a pause, and then a sound that was half a sob and half a laugh, and the board did not lift. "I'm staying here," she said. "Until it's light. You can come up and complain."

*I can't climb the shaft.*

"Then complain from down there."

So he did, a little, about his back, until he heard her breathing slow against the boards. Then he tucked the red thread into the blue cloth at his throat, beside the silver shaving, and lay in the trough, and drew earth qi into his aching spine one slow breath at a time, and did not sleep.

---

The hour of the rabbit came grey and bitter. Ling Mei went to the well with two buckets and a face like a funeral. Romeo watched from the ditch reeds at the end of the back-door tunnel, which was as far as his back would carry him, and counted. One. Two.

On the third bucket, a man came out of the dark between the storehouses and stopped beside her at the well. Masked. Lacquer, leather, horse, silver. A soldier's old sweat, and a night's worth of fear gone stale.

The still escort lowered his own bucket beside hers and did not look at her. He spoke so low that only the girl and a dog with very good ears could have heard it.

"Tell your dog I didn't open the door either," he said. "And tell him: the drain under the kennels. Tonight. He comes alone."

Then he drew up his water and walked away, and Ling Mei stood at the well with the frozen rope in her hands and turned, very slowly, to look straight at the patch of reeds where Romeo was lying.

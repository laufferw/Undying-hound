# **Chapter 27: Below the Grating**

She did not come to the reeds. She was not a fool. She drew her bucket up hand over hand, poured it into the second, and walked back along the lane with both of them sloshing, and as she passed the ditch she said to the air, quite pleasantly, "You heard all that."

*I have very good ears.*

"I know. They're very long." She set the buckets down at the water butt behind the kitchens, tipped them in, and stood for a moment with her hands on the small of her back, which was where Romeo's back hurt too. "He said alone."

*He did.*

"You're not going."

*I am going,* Romeo said, *but not yet. First you are going to draw twenty-one more buckets, and I am going to tell you what was in the den. Then we will argue. Then I will go.*

"That's not an argument. That's a schedule."

*At my age they are the same thing.*

---

He told her over the next three buckets, keeping pace with her below the lip of the bank in a determined low shuffle through the dead reeds, like an old man going to fetch the post. He told her about the shape at the bottom of the shaft, too tall for the room. The hand that had rested under her, and not pushed. Shen's voice. *Romeo,* said the river way.

He told her about the "Sit."

She stopped in the lane with a full bucket in each hand. "It made you sit?"

*It nearly did.* He was quiet a moment. *My first master laughed when he told me to sit. A small laugh, at the end. Every time, for years. He thought it was funny, giving orders to a dog. As if I was going to take them.*

"And this one didn't."

*No. I have heard one other voice say it without the laugh.* He did not say where. There would be a fire and a sausage one day, and that story would need both. *Keep walking. Your arms will set.*

She walked. Water slopped onto the snow and froze there in little glassy tongues. "And then it used your voice," she said. "You sound very sure of yourself. Out loud."

*I am very sure of myself.*

"I know. It was a good imitation. I nearly laughed." She tipped the buckets, and was quiet all the way back to the well.

---

By the tenth bucket the sun had come up white over the long shoulder of the mountain, and her tremor had come up with it. The rope shook. The bucket knocked the well wall going down and swung coming up. Romeo watched from the reeds and could do nothing about it, and this was a feeling he had not got used to in three thousand years: being small, with a bad back, in a ditch, while someone he had decided to look after did something heavy.

He tried anyway. When the junior steward went into the storehouse to count turnips, Romeo came up out of the reeds, took the free end of the well rope in his teeth and braced, to take a little of the weight.

His back made a remark that was not private at all.

"Let go," said Ling Mei.

*I am helping.*

"You're hanging off it. I'm lifting a bucket and a sausage."

*I am stabilising the line.*

"Romeo." She did not say it the river way. She said it the way she said it, flat and fond, Azure Wolf vowels. "Go and lie in your hole."

He let go. He did not go and lie in his hole, because he was a dachshund, and on principle. He lay on top of the bank instead, in the sun, where he could see the well and the kennels and the guest hall all at once, and he put his chin on his paws and pretended this had been his idea.

Romeo looked at the blue panel for a long time. Then he closed his eyes so that he did not have to.

---

At the hour of the snake she had her twelve, by a bucket's margin, and the junior steward made a mark in the bucket book and said, "You look terrible," and gave her half a boiled turnip, which she split with the ditch. Romeo ate his half and found that it was the best thing he had eaten in his life, which happens when one has not eaten.

In the afternoon, between buckets, they argued.

"He's one of the men who guards the stone," Ling Mei said. "He takes their names. You said so. He puts them on that pin."

*I did say so.*

"And now he wants you in a drain. Alone. At night. With your back."

*Yes.*

"Romeo, that is what a trap looks like. That is the picture they would paint in a book, if they were teaching children about traps."

*I smelled him at the well. Traps smell of patience, and of being pleased with yourself. He smelled of nothing but fear. And he said "the door." Something knocked for him too, in somebody's voice, and he didn't open.*

"Then what does he want?"

*I don't know. That's why I'm going.* And then, because she had sat on a board all night with a hand underneath her, and it seemed to him she had earned something more than a schedule, he told her about coat names.

He told her how the stone bit: not flesh, not qi, but the name a beast answered to, said over it while it touched the silver. He told her about Smoke, and the other name under Smoke's coat, never spoken. How a name that has never been said aloud cannot be bitten, because there is nothing for the teeth to catch on.

She put the bucket down.

"Do you have one?" she said. "Under 'Romeo'?"

He could have lied. She did not have the nose to catch him.

*Yes,* he said.

She was quiet. Then she picked up the bucket again, and her face did something complicated and settled on stern. "Don't tell me it."

*I wasn't going to.*

"Good. Don't ever. Not if I ask. Not if *you* ask, out loud, from under the floor." She hefted the bucket and walked on and said, over her shoulder, "I'm going to think about whether I'd like one."

---

He lay on the trough that afternoon, slid in rather than climbed in, and drew earth qi along his spine a vertebra at a time, and he did, for an hour, sleep.

In the sleep, an old man sat in a doorway with a straw hat across his knees and a needle in his mouth.

The brim had come away on one side where a young dog had carried it out of a river, and then, being a young dog, had shaken it. Shen squinted. He licked the thread and missed the needle's eye, and swore, and licked it and missed again. The thread was red. He'd had it off a peddler for a fistful of wild garlic and thought himself very sharp for it.

"Sit," he said, not looking up, because the dog was trying to help by putting its nose in the needle. "Sit. Ha."

The dog sat.

"Look at that," said Shen, and laughed at it properly, and got the thread through at last. "Look at you. Sitting. Who'd have thought."

Romeo woke with his paws twitching and his nose in the dirt of the trough and the smell of onion so close he nearly turned his head for it. It was the thread at his throat, only that, in the blue cloth. He lay still a while longer and let it come to his nose.

---

Ling Mei drew her twenty-fourth bucket at dusk with both arms shaking and no feeling in her hands, and the junior steward wrote it in the book and said, "Well," which was as close as the junior steward came to applause.

In the hut she sat on the third floorboard again, with her blanket and the cold charcoal bowl, and spoke down through the crack.

"If you don't come back—"

*I will come back.*

"You keep saying things like that as though you know."

He did know, after a fashion, in a way that would take a fire and a sausage. *I come back,* he said. *Sometimes it takes a week.*

"That isn't funny."

*No,* he agreed, and went.

---

The back-door tunnel to the ditch: forty paces, level. The ditch: frozen hard, a skin of new snow on old, his trench from the night before half drifted in. He went along it on his belly with his ears spread back and his nose a finger above the ice. At the end of the ditch, where it met the old wall of the kennel yard, the square stone drain opened its mouth, a hole the exact height of a dog that did not need to be any taller.

He went in.

It was black and smelled of wolf: wet fur, straw, the iron of chains, and through the grating at the far end the thin heat of big animals sleeping. Grey Nine, his name gone thin as old cloth. The fox, sour with fear, in the last stall on the left. And over all of it, a cold blue-white weight that was Brow.

Halfway along the drain, in the dark, sat a man.

He had to sit. The drain was not built for men, and he had folded himself into it with his knees against his chest and his spear laid along the floor beside him, butt toward Romeo, point away. Lacquer, leather, horse, silver. Old sweat. And the fear, fresher now, a day's worth, banked up like snow against a door.

He had taken off his mask. Romeo could smell the skin of a face, and that the face was not young.

"You came," the still escort said. His hoarse soldier's voice was barely a breath. "Alone?"

Romeo did not answer, because it was true.

"You can understand me. I know you can. I've watched you." The man's breath shook. "I won't touch you. I'm not here for that. I'm here because—" He stopped. He laughed, and it was not at all like Shen's. "It came to my door, the night before last. It knocked the way my mother knocked. It called me what she called me, out in the barley, when I was small. And I didn't open, I don't know how I didn't open, and in the morning I went to say it to myself, the name she called me. To be sure."

He held out his bare right hand in the dark. Romeo could smell the palm: silver-burn, and the frayed edges of other creatures' names, wolf and fox and stoat, worked into the creases like dirt into a farmer's hands. And under all of that, very faint, leaning uphill like smoke in a draught, the man's own.

"I can't remember it," the escort whispered. "I lay them on the pin every night, what I'm ordered to, and something's been coming off me too. Off my hand. And now I can't remember what my mother called me. You can smell them. They say your kind can smell anything." His voice cracked down the middle. "Tell me my name, dog."

Romeo put his nose to the cold air above the palm, and breathed in slowly, through the nose only.

It was nearly gone. But a name is a smell before it is a word, and Romeo had been reading smells since before this man's grandmother's grandmother was born. Barley. A woman's voice calling across a field at dusk. The sound she made was short and bright and rising, and it had the shape, in the old script that nobody living could read, of a single character: millet, the small grain, the one poor families sowed when the barley failed.

He did not speak mind-to-mind with men in this body. He was not going to start in a drain.

So he did what a dog does. He scratched.

His claw went into the silt on the floor of the drain, and his back complained, and he did not stop. Six strokes, old-fashioned, the way they had been cut into bamboo three thousand years ago. Then he took the man's sleeve in his teeth and drew the bare hand down to the marks.

The escort traced them. Once. Twice. He did not know the script, but something in him did; his hand knew before he did, the way a tail knows.

"Shu," he said. "Ah-Shu." And then he put his face against his knees and wept without any sound at all, the way soldiers learn to.

Romeo waited. Old dogs are good at waiting.

At last the man lifted his head. "I owe you," he said. "So listen. Your old man. The rest of him. It's true. Up the dry passage, past where the water stops, there's a room in the rock. It keeps things there. Not names; the names go to the pin. Things it *wears*. Shoes. Voices. A walk, hung up like a coat on a peg." He swallowed. "I've seen a straw hat on that wall."

Romeo did not move. His tail, in the dark, did, once.

"On the new moon," Ah-Shu went on, faster now, "the collector goes to the stone, and I'm to go up the passage with the last of the edges, and the room will be open. It's the only night it's open. I can take you. There and back, and nobody looks at the bottom of a wall." "That's all I want. To give you that, and then get out of these mountains before I forget anything else."

It was a good offer. It smelled almost entirely of fear and gratitude, and Romeo had smelled worse men tell the truth.

Above them, at the end of the drain, the grating darkened.

Something enormous had lowered its head to the bars. Cold came down through them, snow-cold, blue-white, and a pair of eyes the colour of ice under moonlight looked down the length of the drain at the small dog and the folded man.

Brow did not look at the man. He looked only at Romeo, and when he spoke it was in his rusty-gate voice, into Romeo's head alone, so quietly that it was barely more than the smell of snow.

*Small dog,* said Brow. *He is telling you the truth about the hat.*

A pause, long and wintry.

*He is lying to you about the door.*

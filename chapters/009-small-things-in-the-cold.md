# **Chapter 9: Small Things in the Cold**

The pin came down slowly, the way a heron's beak goes into still water.

Romeo watched it come. He could not help watching it; it was a finger's width from his left eye, sliding down through the grey roof of his tunnel with a faint dry hiss of snow parting around metal. First the rice-grain head with its knot of pale silk. Then the shaft, longer than the ones at the springs, as long as his own foreleg, and so cold he could feel it on his whiskers before it reached him. It passed his nose. It passed his chin. It went on down past the floor of his tunnel into the softened line of the seam, and stopped.

Above him, the lantern light did not move.

He had been still before. He had been still under the Beast Hall floor with a Nascent Soul's spiritual sense moving over the boards like a hand over a sleeping face. He had been still for nine days once, in the rafters of a burning monastery, waiting for a fire spirit to lose interest. Stillness was not hard. Stillness was only a matter of deciding that every part of you belonged to the ground.

His nose did not agree.

It began as a tickle, high up behind the bridge of his muzzle, where a grain of salted rice had been lodged since the hour of the goat. He had sneezed most of that rice out at the time. Not all of it. Some small, determined part of his lunch had climbed into the dark of his skull and waited there all afternoon for exactly this moment, and now it stirred.

*No,* Romeo told it.

The tickle spread. His upper lip twitched. His eyes began to water, and the water ran down the white blaze of his muzzle and froze there in two thin lines.

*I have outlived four dynasties,* he informed his nose. *I have held my breath at the bottom of the Weeping River while a dragon counted its eggs. I will not be undone by rice.*

He pressed his muzzle flat against the floor of the tunnel, hard, until the cold of the packed snow went up into the bone, and breathed out through his mouth, very slowly, a dog's thin silent pant. The tickle hung on the edge. Hung there. Then, sullenly, it went back to wherever rice goes.

The lantern light shifted. The snow creaked once above his back, softly, as a person creaks when they change from crouching to kneeling. And then, close enough that he felt it through the roof of his tunnel more than heard it, a voice.

"Hush," it said.

It was not loud. It was not cruel. It was the voice you would use with a fretful child, or a horse at night, or water. It had no age in it that he could find and no accent from any province he knew, and he knew all of them, including three that no longer existed.

Under his chin, the seam went quiet.

Not silent. It still ran; he could smell it running, clay and cold and earth qi sliding uphill to downhill under the snow. But it ran the way a dog walks at heel. He felt it go, the way you feel a room change when someone important comes in, and every hair along the red saddle of his back stood up against the roof of the tunnel and brushed snow down onto his spine.

He did not move. He did not know, could not know, whether that word had been for the water or for him.

He breathed in instead. Very small. Very careful. He reached past the silk and the lamp smoke and the silver for the one thing the Heavenly Nose had never once failed to find in any living creature that stood near him: intent. What it wanted. Hunger smells, and anger, and fear, and kindness, and greed, and even boredom has its own flat, dusty scent.

The person kneeling above him smelled of nothing at all.

Not hidden. Romeo knew hidden; hidden smelled like a lid. This was empty, the way the space under a bell is empty. Whatever stood over him in the snow wanted something, it must, it had climbed a mountain with a lantern and a bundle of silver pins, but whatever it wanted was not anywhere a nose could reach.

For three thousand years, Romeo had found that there were very few things worse than a creature who could lie to his nose. He had just found one.

The lantern light lifted. The snow above his back eased as the weight came off it, what little weight there was. The footsteps went away, not down the mountain but up it again, toward the saddle, light and even. They did not hurry. They did not look back. Or if they looked back, they did it without turning, which some people could.

Only when he could no longer hear them did the voice come again, from a long way off, carried down on the wind so faintly that he would forever afterwards be unsure whether he had heard it or smelled it.

"Cold night," it said, "for small things."

---

In the outer courtyard of the Azure Wolf Sect, at the same dusk, a junior steward stood in the doorway of a disciple's hut with a lamp in one hand and his sleeve over his nose.

"Elder Fang asks after the dog," he said.

Ling Mei, who had been sitting on the floor beside the third floorboard from the wall with a bowl of something brown and steaming in her lap, looked up at him with an expression of enormous fatigue. "He's under the floor."

"Still?"

"Still." She lifted the bowl. "Bitter melon and burnt rice. The cook says it binds the bowels. He won't come up for it. Would you like to try?"

The steward looked at the floorboard. The floorboard, she knew, smelled exactly like what it was supposed to smell like. She had made sure of that this morning, with a cupful of last night's slops and the end of the drain ditch and a strength of character she had not previously known she possessed.

"Elder Fang," the steward said, still through his sleeve, "wishes to know whether the animal is eating."

It was a strange question. She heard how strange it was, and she made her face do nothing about it. A dog that was eating was a dog that was alive and well and under the floor. A dog that was not eating might be a dog that was not there.

"He ate four rice balls yesterday," she said, which was true, in a way. "That's probably what did it."

The steward nodded slowly. He wrote nothing down, which worried her more than if he had. Then he said, "The naming is the day after tomorrow, at the hour of the snake. Every beast. Elder Fang was very clear. Even sick ones," and went away with his lamp across the frozen courtyard toward the Beast Hall.

Ling Mei set the bowl down. She looked at her hands in the last of the light.

The right one was shaking. It had shaken since she was eleven, a small fine tremor like a leaf in a draught, and for two nights it had been almost still. Tonight it was back, and worse, and when she closed her eyes she could feel the thing the dog called her tide moving up the inside of her arms, slow and heavy, pressing at banks it had only just learned it had.

"Back by the morning of the seventh day," she said to the empty hole under the floor.

The hole, being a hole, said nothing. She lay down next to it anyway, with her shaking hand over the gap in the boards, as if something in the dark down there might come up and lick it.

---

Romeo came out of the snow at full dark, because a tunnel was no use to him any longer.

Below the saddle, the wind had scoured the slope down to bare grey rock and old ice, and the seam ran beneath it where no dog could dig. He stood on the stone and shook himself, ears first, then body, then tail, and a great deal of the mountain left him in a white spray. A great deal more stayed. His ears had frozen into two stiff curls, like dried leaves. His paws had gone past numb into something that did not belong to him.

*Recommendation noted,* said Romeo. *Find me some.*

The System did not find him any. It seldom did.

He untied the blue bundle with teeth and nose and one useless frozen paw, and looked at the last two rice balls for a long time. Then he ate one, slowly, chewing on the side of his mouth that still had feeling, and tied the other back up with what he privately considered the finest knot of his life. It held on the second try. He did not sneeze.

One left. Night of Day 5\. When he called the quest timer it said **\[1 day, 12 hours\]**, and he did not do the sum again. He knew the sum. The sum was that he had no more time to stand on rocks admiring knots.

The stranger's tracks went up over the saddle. Even on bare stone he could follow them, not by print but by scent, silk and lamp smoke laid on the wind like a thread through a needle's eye. He followed them because they went where the seam went, and he had no other road, and because a small hard part of him that had been a cultivator for three thousand years wanted very badly to see what kind of thing had said *hush* to a stream and been obeyed.

The climb to the saddle was short and terrible. The rock went up in ledges, each one about the height of the sleeping platform in Ling Mei's hut, and he had never yet got onto the sleeping platform. He did not get onto these either, not the way a goat would have. He found the cracks between them, and the places where ice had split the stone, and he went up through the mountain the way water comes down it, low, in the folds, his long belly scraping every edge. Twice his back legs slid out from under him and he hung by his front claws with his hind end swinging over nothing, and both times he thought of his spine, the long weak line of it from shoulder to hip that had broken once, nine hundred years ago, in a fall from a much lower wall than this.

Both times he got a hind paw onto something and pushed.

At the top of the saddle the wind hit him side-on, hard enough to lift his ears out from his head like two red flags. He flattened himself between two stones and looked down.

The lake lay in a bowl between the peaks, perhaps half a li across, black as ink under the new moon. Ice rimmed its edges in a pale band. In the middle the water was open and perfectly still, so still it held the stars, and the smell that came up off it was so old and so deep that for a moment Romeo forgot the cold entirely. Black mud. Moss that had forgotten it was green. Stone that had been under water since before there were sects. And beneath it all, slow as the turning of the year, old shell.

The seam ran down the far side of the saddle and into the lake. He could smell it go. It did not start there; it was the lake, reaching out under the mountain like a root.

He should have felt something like triumph. He felt it start to come, and then he saw the lantern.

It was down on the shore, on the near side, a small yellow bead of light at the edge of the ice. Beside it knelt a tall, narrow figure in pale silk, facing the water. He could not see a face. He could see the figure's hands moving in the lantern light, quick and patient, like a woman at a loom.

And then, because his eyes were a dog's eyes and better in the dark than any human's, he saw the threads.

They came in from every direction across the snow. One from the seam behind him, running down from the saddle past his own frozen paws. Others from the east and the west, from valleys he had not climbed, from other seams he had not followed: a dozen pale lines of silk, then two dozen, faint as spider-thread in the starlight, all converging on that small bead of light at the water's edge. The figure gathered them in its quick hands. Knotted them. Fed them, one by one, out across the ice and down into the open black water, toward the deep, still place in the middle of the lake where the smell of old shell was strongest.

Romeo lay between the stones on the top of the saddle, with one rice ball and no feeling in his feet, and understood at last what the pins were for.

Someone was not putting a leash on a stream.

Someone was putting a leash on a turtle.

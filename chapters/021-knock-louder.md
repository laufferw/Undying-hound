# **Chapter 21: Knock Louder**

Three knocks. Two. Louder.  
Then nothing, and the nothing went on, and Romeo lay with his chest against the cold rock beside the spring and listened to it go on.  
*If it's the end of the world, knock louder.* Bao had said it lying in the mud of a second-tier lake bed with a waterweed draped over one eye. Romeo had taken it for a joke. But Bao did not make jokes. Bao made remarks, very slowly, over the course of a century, and some of them turned out to be funny if you were still alive at the end.  
He took his chest off the rock and looked at the crack. It was as wide as it had been an hour ago, which was to say wide enough for a whisker and nothing that came after the whisker. He looked uphill: the black pines, the long white shoulder of the mountain, and somewhere beyond it a lake under ice and an old friend under the lake. Overland, in this snow, on these legs, it would take the rest of the night and most of what was left of him. And the stranger had gone that way first, walking a dry passage where there was no snow at all.  
He looked downhill. Somewhere down there, under a hut with a floorboard that sat a little proud, a girl was asleep with her boot dragged across the doorway.  
For three thousand years Romeo had done the urgent things alone, because the people he might have woken were dead. He had become very good at it. It had also, by his own careful count, got him killed eleven times.  
*She will be cross,* he thought. *She will be cross either way. Better cross and there.*  
He turned round in the snow, which for a dachshund on a slope is a manoeuvre in several parts, and went down.  
---

Going down was faster. It was not more dignified.  
The crust beside the escort's boot-holes would just bear him if he spread himself out, so he did: chin down, ears out, legs folded under, and let the mountain have him. He went down the slope on his belly like a small red plough that had lost its farmer, steering with his ears and once, briefly, with his face. A drift caught him at the bottom and stopped him dead, and he lay inside it for a moment with snow in his mouth, thinking about the Weeping River dragon, which had at least been warm.  
The escort's trail went on ahead of him, down between the last two storehouses and across the corner of the outer yard. Romeo put his nose to it at the edge of the buildings and followed it with his eyes shut. Fear, gone stale now. Silver. The river, very faint. The trail went to the door of the guest hall and through it, and stopped.  
*Gone to bed,* Romeo thought. *Or gone to wake the collector and tell him a small dog was out on the hill tonight.* The smell could not tell him which. Smells were very good at where and very bad at why.  
He went the long way round anyway, by the ditch, and into the reeds, and down the back-door tunnel, which was dry and dark and exactly the right size for him. He felt himself unclench in it, the whole length of his spine at once. Then the round chamber, the trough breathing its faint warmth of earth qi, and the shaft. He climbed the shaft the way he always climbed it, which is to say badly, and put the top of his head against the third floorboard and pushed.  
The board lifted. Light came in round its edges. Not moonlight. Something small and red.  
Ling Mei was awake.  
---

She sat on the floor with her back against the sleeping platform and her blanket round her shoulders. The boot was in her lap. The contraband charcoal was lit in a chipped clay bowl beside her, a single coal glowing like an eye, and she was looking straight at the floorboard, as if she had been looking at it for some time.  
Romeo came up out of the floor. He was soaked to the skin. There was snow packed in both ears, a crust of old blood over one eye, and three pine needles still standing in his nose.  
"Where," said Ling Mei, "have you been?"  
He had prepared for this. All the way down the hill he had prepared for it.  
"You smell," she went on, "like a wet sock that somebody dragged through a pine tree."  
*I had prepared,* said Romeo, with dignity, *for "old boot."*  
"That's coming."  
He shook himself. It was a long dog and the shake took a long time to travel down it, and when it reached his tail most of the hut was wet. Ling Mei did not move. Her right hand, he saw, was trembling on the boot. It had been still when he left.  
So he told her. He told it fast, mind to mind, with no grace and nothing left out: the kennel drain, the fox in her cage and the silver hasp, the still escort and his bare hand full of stolen names, the pin, the tapping in the crack, the thing that stood inside the hill and smelled of nothing living. The tree well. *Go home, dog.* And then the knocking, three and then two, from inside the mountain, and then again, louder.  
When he had finished she was quiet for a while.  
He breathed her in, because he could not help it. Fear first, sharp and clean, the way it always was with her, never sour. Then something under the fear, slower and heavier, like iron cooling on an anvil.  
"You went up the mountain," she said. "Alone. After a Hound-Catcher."  
*After a Hound-Catcher's escort. It's a different thing.*  
"Is it."  
*Slightly.*  
She put the boot down. "And now you're going to the lake."  
*We are,* said Romeo.  
She looked at him. He found, to his annoyance, that he had to look at the charcoal instead.  
*I was at the spring,* he said. *The lake is uphill from the spring. I could have gone on.* His tail moved once against the floor, without asking. *I have done the end of the world on my own before. Several times. I didn't care for it.*  
Ling Mei said nothing at all. She took the blanket off her shoulders, picked him up in it, and began to rub him dry, hard, the way you would dry a dog that had come out of a river. She did his ears, which he permitted. She did his back, carefully, along the long ache of it, which he permitted with his eyes half shut. She did his belly, and his left hind leg began to kick in the air entirely of its own accord.  
*That is a reflex,* said Romeo. *It means nothing.*  
"Of course it doesn't." She set him down on the blanket and stood up and began pulling on her boots. "How far is it?"  
*For you, the rest of the night. For me, through that snow...* He considered it honestly. *Some time next spring.*  
"Then you're not walking."  
*I am not luggage.*  
"You're shorter than the snow."  
*I am the correct length,* said Romeo. *The snow is wrong.*  
"Then the snow can apologise to you later." She shrugged on her padded outer coat, left it open at the front, scooped him up and put him inside it against her chest, and closed it over him so that only his head stuck out under her chin. Her left arm curled beneath him to hold him there. It curled easily. It was the arm that would not straighten.  
"This arm's no good for much," she said. "It's good for this."  
Romeo opened his mouth to say something ancient and dry, and found that for once he had nothing.  
---

They went out by the door, like honest people, and round behind the hut, and up the back of the storehouses where the shadow lay thickest. Ling Mei kept well away from the escort's boot-holes without being told. The guest hall stood dark. Nothing moved in the yard but the wind and the assessment stone's silver band, which did not move but caught what little light there was and seemed to.  
Above the black pines the climb began in earnest.  
It was a different mountain from inside a coat. Romeo had walked a great many roads in his lives, but he had very seldom been carried along one, and he found it humbling to discover how much of the world simply went by if you were not fighting it with your legs. Ling Mei did the fighting. She went into drifts to the knee and then to the thigh, and hauled herself out, and went on. Her breath came short and white past his ears. He could feel her heart through the padded cloth, and under her heart the strange backward pull of her tide, an Ebbtide Vessel turning slowly against the cold the way deep water turns under ice.  
He did the only thing he could do. He kept his head out in the freezing air and gave her his nose.  
*Left,* he said. *Rock under the snow there. Not that left. The other left. Good.*  
"There is only one left."  
*There are many lefts, and you keep finding the wrong one. Up. That's a hare's track; the hare knows where the crust is firm. Follow the hare.*  
She followed the hare. The hare had known.  
Twice he stopped her with a word and they stood silent in the dark while he read the wind. Once it was only a fox, an ordinary one, unbound and unawakened, going about its business and smelling of mouse. The second time it was nothing he could name: a thread of lamp smoke very far off to the east, there and gone, as if a door had opened somewhere inside the mountain and closed again.  
"Is it the stranger?" she whispered.  
*It's under us,* said Romeo. *Whatever it is. Keep going.*  
Somewhere on the long white shoulder of the mountain, with her arm under him and her heartbeat in his ribs and the cold kept out by her coat, Romeo did a thing he had not done properly in two days. He fell asleep.

He was aware of it in a vague way, a blue glow at the edge of a dream about a river. *Don't tell her,* he thought at the System, and slept on.  
---

He woke because she had stopped.  
The dark had gone grey. The wind had dropped. Ling Mei stood at the top of a last white rise, swaying a little, breathing hard, and below them, in a bowl of black rock and snow, lay the lake.  
It was frozen from shore to shore. The ice was the colour of an old knife and quite unbroken, and the snow on it lay smooth and trackless all the way across. No hole. No crack. No great shape rising out of the water. Nothing moved anywhere in the whole bowl of the mountain, and the stillness of it went into Romeo like cold water down the back of the neck.  
*Put me down,* he said.  
She put him down. The snow came up to his chest. He went through it anyway, the way his ancestors had gone into badger earths, low and stubborn and without the least interest in what anyone thought of his chances, down the slope to the jumble of boulders at the water's edge.  
The smell found him before he found it. Silk. Lamp smoke. Silver. It came up out of the rocks at the foot of the biggest boulder, where a low black slot ran in under the stone. It was nothing like the hairline crack at the spring. It was wide. It was dark. It was, Romeo saw with the part of him that had been bred for exactly this, almost precisely the size of a dachshund.  
The other end of the dry passage.  
His hackles rose. His tail came up stiff. Every badger-hound who had ever lived stood up inside him and said *in*, and his front paws were already on the lip of the slot before Ling Mei's hand closed round his tail.  
"Don't you dare," she said.  
And from inside the slot, very close now, no more than a few body-lengths into the dark, something knocked on the stone.  
Three knocks.  
Then two.  
Romeo did not breathe. Behind him Ling Mei did not breathe. And then, from out on the lake, from somewhere deep beneath that smooth grey ice, a voice he had known for three thousand years spoke into his mind, very faint and very slow and more frightened than he had ever heard it.  
*Romeo. Little brother. Don't answer that.*  
A long pause, the way Bao always paused.  
*I haven't knocked on anything in four hundred years. That isn't me.*
